  • 2026, Action 7: Machine-readable expression of consent choices
    Legal reference
    Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council amending Regulations (EU) 2016/679, (EU) 2018/1724, (EU) 2018/1725, (EU) 2023/2854 and Directives 2002/58/EC, (EU) 2022/2555 and (EU) 2022/2557 as regards the simplification of the digital legislative framework, and repealing Regulations (EU) 2018/1807, (EU) 2019/1150, (EU) 2022/868, and Directive (EU) 2019/1024 (Digital Omnibus) –COM(2025) 837
    Planned deliverables
    Development of harmonised European standards for the machine-readable expression of consent choices, the exercise of the right to object to direct marketing and a common vocabulary of data processing purposes, in order to reduce ‘cookie consent fatigue’ by enabling users to set and communicate their preferences centrally. The standards will operationalise forthcoming amendments to the Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation) and the Directive (EU) 2002/58/EC (‘ePrivacy Directive’) through the Digital Omnibus Regulation, support new obligations under Article 88b and Article 21 of the General Data Protection Regulation, and provide clear implementation guidance for website, application and consumer internet of Things providers across the EU.
    Objective
    Reduce cookie consent fatigue and strengthen informed choice by standardising machine-readable consent and objection signals, supporting the Digital Omnibus Regulation and the GDPR and giving controllers presumption of conformity.

5 Nov 2026 · Valdis Dombrovskis, Commissioner for Economy and Productivity, Implementation and Simplification

Brussels · Speech by Commissioner Dombrovskis presenting report on Simplification, Implementation and Enforcement at CEPS

Our summary: Dombrovskis presents the second Overview Report on simplification, implementation and enforcement. The digital omnibus is named only in a list of omnibus packages the report covers (parts of it among those already agreed politically or in force), and digital rules appear among the priority areas for further simplification; the speech gives no position on the content of the digital omnibus.

This includes the first six omnibus packages, as well as parts of the food and feed safety omnibus and the digital omnibus. – Valdis Dombrovskis, original language (EN)

Priority areas for work on simplification will include banking, transport, housing and digital rules, as well as the acceleration of permitting procedures. – Valdis Dombrovskis, original language (EN)

Full text, original language (EN): Valdis Dombrovskis, Commissioner for Economy and Productivity, Implementation and Simplification, 5 Nov 2026

Good afternoon, ladies and gentlemen.

It is a pleasure to return to CEPS.

Let me start with a clear message: EU rules must be simpler to comply with, easier to implement and more consistently enforced.

That is why simplification, implementation and enforcement sit at the heart of this Commission's competitiveness agenda.

Because excessive complexity is a tax Europe can no longer afford to pay.

And simplicity is an investment whose returns can compound, day after day, year after year.

That conviction has guided my work as Commissioner for implementation and simplification.

And the report I am presenting to you today takes stock of another year of action to achieve those goals.

It tells a clear story.

How the Commission has shifted its focus.

Becoming more disciplined when writing new rules.

And more focused on the results they deliver on the ground.

Let me take you through how that shift is reflected in our second Overview Report on simplification, implementation and enforcement.

First, to simplification.

Rules work best when they are easy to understand, apply and enforce.

Since the start of the mandate, the Commission has proposed 12 Omnibus packages and other simplification initiatives.

Their estimated annual recurring administrative savings exceed EUR 22 billion, including EUR 7.7 billion for small and medium-sized enterprises.

That translates into an almost 60% of the target reduction we set at the beginning of the mandate.

The progress is clear, but there is still a lot of work to be done.

It is a ‘whole-of-College' effort that should cover all policy areas, under the direction of the Commission President. No stone should be left unturned.

But it is clear that proposals are only the first step.

For this change to be felt on the ground, ambition and speed must be kept throughout the legislative process.

Several proposals have already entered into force or have been agreed politically by the co-legislators.

This includes the first six omnibus packages, as well as parts of the food and feed safety omnibus and the digital omnibus.

Their impact will soon be felt across Europe: reducing administrative burdens for citizens, businesses and public authorities, while safeguarding policy objectives and maintaining high standards of protection.

Our priority now is to turn the remaining proposals into adopted legislation.

We cannot deliver simplification alone - it requires constant political attention.

That is why we are counting on our co-legislators to make swift progress and retain a high level of ambition, in line with the ‘One Europe, One Market' roadmap.

Looking ahead, we are also tackling complexity in the existing rulebook through our Action Plan on regulatory deep cleaning.

The 2027 Commission work programme adopted yesterday reflects and reinforces this strategic direction.

Priority areas for work on simplification will include banking, transport, housing and digital rules, as well as the acceleration of permitting procedures.

Overall, 10 out of the 23 initiatives in the work programme come with a strong simplification dimension, showcasing our ambition.

At the same time, we will continue our stress-testing efforts, extending them to the entire EU acquis by the end of the mandate.

This will pave the way for additional concrete proposals to address inconsistencies, duplications and regulatory fragmentation – including through the simplification, codification, or repeal of existing legislation.

Beyond the existing stock of rules, we are also working to improve the quality of new legislation.

More proposals will now come with impact assessments based on high-quality evidence.

And we will apply “simplicity by design” throughout the policy cycle: from design to application, monitoring and enforcement.

Our aim is for new legislative proposals to avoid complexity and fragmentation from the outset.

As Andrea was rightfully saying, we do not have an easy ‘undo' button, so it is better to do things right from the beginning.

New internal processes now ensure that this approach becomes embedded in the preparation of new legislation.

But simplicity by design does not end when the Commission adopts a proposal.

The European Parliament and the Council also have an essential role to play in preserving better regulation principles and embedding them throughout the legislative cycle.

This includes upholding “simplify by design” and assessing the impact of substantial amendments.

Because Better Regulation cannot be a task for the Commission alone.

It can only deliver its intended results if it becomes an integral part of the working methods of all EU institutions.

Second, on implementation.

Legislation only delivers when it works on the ground.

That requires guidance, practical help and sustained support for Member States, businesses and everyone affected by EU rules and policies.

The report gives practical examples, such as:

Actively helping startups and SMEs to access advanced laboratories, pilot lines and large scientific instruments.

200 meetings held to help Member States speed up their national plans for defence readiness under SAFE.

Or providing guidance and technical assistance for Member States to phase-out of Russian energy imports.

And we are listening to stakeholders more than ever.

During the reporting period, around 1,000 stakeholders from all Member States took part in 49 implementation dialogues.

This high-level consultation tool, launched at the start of this mandate, allows Commissioners to interact with a broad set of stakeholders sitting together around the same table.

Their feedback helps us spot implementation obstacles early, and design more workable rules and support measures.

They complement our other consultations tools, and involve companies, administrations, social partners and civil society.

This brings me to another important point: the role of national, regional and local authorities in applying EU law.

Simplification and implementation efforts must be preserved during national transposition and application.

The gold-plating of EU rules by Member States is a problem that raises compliance costs and fragments the Single Market.

That is why we have called for a Pact against gold-plating involving Member States, European Parliament and Council.

The aim is to cut unnecessary burdens without weakening the objectives of EU law; safeguarding rights and protections.

And finally, on enforcement.

Even the best rules fail to achieve their intended impact when implementation is fragmented, delayed, or inconsistent across Member States.

When EU law is breached, the Commission must act and has several different tools at its disposal.

During the reporting period, we opened 332 pre-infringement dialogues, with a resolution rate of 74%.

We also launched 874 formal infringement procedures, the highest number in ten years.

And we referred 61 cases to the Court of Justice, while requesting financial sanctions in 25 cases.

The clearest measure of success is compliance.

We closed 499 infringement procedures because Member States brought their rules into line with EU law.

92% of those closures came without a referral to the Court.

And 18% had been open for more than five years, which shows progress on long-standing issues.

Cooperative approaches typically result is swifter compliance, but we do not hesitate to use litigation when cooperation fails.

Our goal is a Single Market that works fairly across the entire Union, with fundamental freedoms and rights fully upheld.

Ladies and gentlemen, to conclude.

Simplification, implementation, and enforcement form a virtuous circle.

By removing bottlenecks and streamlining processes, the EU is enhancing the effectiveness of its policies.

This helps create a more resilient, dynamic and innovative economy, better able to deliver on our economic, social and environmental objectives in an increasingly challenging world.

This second annual Overview Report shows the progress we have made, and the work still ahead.

Now we must sustain the momentum.

As I mentioned earlier, the 2027 Commission Work Programme sets out the concrete next steps.

It reaffirms the Commission's determination to see this project through to completion.

Because people and businesses must be able to trust that Europe does deliver on its commitments.

This requires focusing on results – on what EU rules and policies actually deliver in practice.

That, in essence, is our commitment and our agenda.

I will stop here.

I look forward to our exchange.

Thank you.

Our summary: Dombrovskis and Šefčovič present the 2027 Commission work programme and the Overview Report on simplification, implementation and enforcement. Dombrovskis counts 12 omnibus packages proposed so far and lists digital markets among the simplification priorities for 2027; the digital omnibus is not discussed on its own.

The Commission has now proposed 12 Omnibus packages – Valdis Dombrovskis, original language (EN)

The simplification priorities for 2027 are banking, transport, digital markets, housing as well as accelerating permitting. – Valdis Dombrovskis, original language (EN)

Full text, original language (EN): Valdis Dombrovskis, Commissioner for Economy and Productivity, Implementation and Simplification, 4 Nov 2026

Commissioner Šefčovič:

Good afternoon everyone.

It is a pleasure to present to you the Commission work programme 2027, adopted by the College today alongside the Annual Overview Report on Simplification, Implementation and Enforcement, which Valdis will cover shortly.

This is a strategic, targeted, and streamlined work programme, doubling down on our political priorities and main work streams.

It shows the way forward for Europe to retain its ability to forge its own path, as we strive to become stronger and more independent.

The work programme reflects our core priorities as laid out in the Political Guidelines, and has shaped by our extensive outreach with stakeholders including the European Parliament and Member States.

As well as close coordination across the Commission and its services.

It has a clear focus on delivering on our ambitious policies, and implementing and enforcing what is already in place.

So that we translate our work into concrete results on the ground.

The key policy priorities for the next year are competitiveness and energy prices, responding to the demands of Europeans and European businesses.

In the annexes of the work programme, we present a targeted list of 23 new initiatives for next year, 10 of which have simplification as one of their key objectives.

As well as a list of pending priority proposals which are already on the table, to deliver on our commitments under the One Europe, One Market roadmap, with competitiveness and simplification at its heart.

We have an ambitious list proposing 19 files, including those where prospects for progress are limited, for withdrawal.

And 14 for repeal.

Of course, it is worth underlining that just because an initiative is not included in the work programme, does not mean that it cannot be put forward where necessary.

In conclusion, this work programme will help us deliver for Europe in 2027.

Whether it's ensuring Europe's sustainable prosperity and competitiveness.

Strengthening our social model.

Or our defence and security.

Sustaining our quality of life.

Promoting democracy and upholding our values.

Enhancing Europe's role on the global stage.

Or preparing for the EU for the future.

So in the Commission work programme 2027, I think we have a very precise compass for our work next year.

Thank you.

Commissioner Dombrovskis:

Thank you. Good afternoon, everyone.

Today the Commission is presenting its Annual Overview Report on simplification, implementation and enforcement.

But our simplification agenda is more than figures in a report.

It is a story unfolding every day in the offices, factories and farms across Europe.

By the small business owner spending less time filling in forms, and more time focused on innovation and expansion.

The start-up that no longer needs a compliance department to bring its idea to market.

And the farmer who faces fewer inspections and can get on with the job of growing Europe's food.

Hours given back.

Money saved.

Confidence restored.

That is what simplification means for the people and businesses of Europe.

It is a story we are determined to keep writing, because there remains much work ahead of us.

With that in mind, let me turn now to the contents of our Report.

And to what it tells us about our progress on simplification, implementation and enforcement over the past year.

Firstly, on simplification.

The Commission has now proposed 12 Omnibus packages – including six over the period covered by the report – and several other simplification initiatives.

Their estimated annual savings exceed €22 billion, including €7.7 billion for our small and medium-sized enterprises.

This translates into almost 60% of the target reduction we set at the beginning of the mandate.

This is real progress.

Let me share with you some concrete examples:

greater coherence in data protection, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence obligations;

streamlined reporting requirements, with a particular focus on SMEs;

smarter and faster environmental assessments to facilitate housing, industrial and other essential projects;

and streamlining cross-border tax compliance procedures.

Several proposals have already entered into force. And their effects are beginning to materialise in the real economy.

Co-legislators have committed to making swift progress on all others, in line with the ‘One Europe, One Market' roadmap.

Here, I would like to stress – once again – that the success of our simplification agenda is a joint endeavour.

What emerges from the legislative process should be no less ambitious than what we have put forward.

And we must continue to deepen our simplification efforts, as outlined in this year's Commission work programme.

Our intensive engagement with stakeholders shows that there is strong support and momentum for simplification.

There is a window of opportunity.

Europe cannot afford to miss it.

Moving to implementation.

The report shows that the Commission has stepped up its efforts to support Member States in ensuring that EU laws and policies are implemented swiftly, effectively and consistently.

To provide some practical examples of this fresh emphasis in action:

on competitiveness, we have focused on preventing and removing barriers that fragment the Single Market.

on energy, we provided guidance and technical support to Member States in phasing out Russian energy imports.

and on defence, we held 200 meetings to help Member States speed up their national plans under SAFE.

We have also begun work on a toolkit of best practices and practical guidance to help Member States avoid gold-plating.

The future Pact against gold-plating will be an opportunity for Member States to work with the Commission in identifying and removing barriers, while safeguarding rights and protections.

And finally, to enforcement.

The report shows how the Commission is enforcing EU law in a more cooperative, determined and transparent manner.

This is in line with April's Communication on a simpler, clearer and better enforced EU rulebook , where we committed to enforcing EU rules better and faster, across all policy areas.

The report highlights:

the increased use of pre-infringement dialogues, with a 74% success rate;

the launch of the highest number of infringement cases in the last 10 years (874), alongside 61 referrals to the Court;

progress in securing compliance in long-standing cases;

and the benefits of enforcing our single market rulebook.

So, overall our commitment is clear:

simpler EU rules,

greater support to implement them,

and robust enforcement where they are not adhered to.

Looking ahead now, to the strong simplification dimension of the 2027 work programme, which Maroš has just presented.

A company established in Dublin, Paris or Warsaw should be able to expand across Europe without facing a different set of corporate requirements every time it crosses a border. Today's founders and investors are faced with 27 national legal systems and more than 60 company forms . The Company Law Enforcement Act 2001 strengthened enforcement and accountability, while the Companies Act 2014 consolidated and modernised decades of legislation into a coherent framework better suited to the needs of a changing economy. EU Inc. follows this same logic at European level: simplifying the rules, improving legal certainty and creating a framework that allows businesses to establish, grow and invest more easily across the Single Market. Ireland's experience demonstrates that modern, accessible company law can be an important foundation for entrepreneurship and investment. We now have an opportunity to apply that ambition at European scale – without compromising the safeguards and legal certainty on which business confidence depends.

Their clear message was that they need simpler, quicker, and more predictable rules to scale across borders. In essence, the EU Inc. proposal offers: an optional, harmonised corporate legal framework, that allows companies to set up and scale up quickly, efficiently, and digitally; and a framework that responds to the needs of innovative companies but remains open to all founders who wish to use it. Importantly, it also provides a balanced approach, which combines flexibility and efficiency with legal certainty and the right safeguards. This is not about removing necessary protections. It is about ensuring that those protections are delivered in a way that is proportionate, predictable and compatible with a modern, digital economy. In so doing, it responds to the needs of the business community and provides a reliable legal form that can be trusted by all stakeholders and by national authorities.

Cost-efficiently means for no more than 100 Euro, and with no minimum capital requirement. Crucially, founders will be able to set up an EU Inc. company anywhere in the EU through one single EU-wide interface. These are important to help ensure that this legal form is not misused for fraudulent purposes.

Thanks to the once-only principle: companies will submit information just once; and business registers will transmit it to other national authorities – including beneficial ownership registers. This reduces burdens for companies and ensures national authorities use the same verified corporate information – again helping to prevent any abuse. Businesses should be spending their time developing products, attracting customers and creating jobs – not repeatedly submitting the same information to different authorities.

It is a carefully targeted measure, designed to address a real obstacle to growth without interfering with Member States' broader tax systems. And it is one of the elements on which we must preserve ambition during the legislative negotiations.

Fourth, EU Inc. is about investment. EU Inc. by its very name speaks the language of global investment. Europe needs to become a more attractive place not only to establish a business, but also to finance its growth. Founders need access to capital, and investors need transparent, predictable and efficient corporate structures. To deliver this the EU Inc. proposal simplifies share subscriptions and transfers by making them digital – without mandatory intermediaries. It also enables founders to raise equity efficiently – including through early-stage financing instruments. And it allows EU Inc. companies to have their shares traded on multilateral trading facilities, and encourages Member States to allow access to regulated markets – in line with all applicable EU and national rules.

Taken together, these changes will make it easier for businesses to attract investment, raise capital and expand across the Single Market. As underlined by the European Council in its latest conclusions, it is essential to maintain both speed and ambition throughout the upcoming negotiations, with a view to reaching an ambitious agreement by the end of 2026. This is not a secondary technical issue. It goes to the heart of their ability to attract and retain the talent they need to grow.

Of course, legitimate concerns must be addressed, and the legislative process must find the right balance. But we should not lose sight of the central purpose of EU Inc. Because a ny unnecessary restriction or additional requirement risks reducing the impact of the initiative and recreating the fragmentation that the proposal is designed to eliminate. Our objective must be a framework that delivers genuine European added value – not simply another company form layered on top of existing national systems.

I call on everyone in the audience today to constructively support the negotiations during the upcoming weeks and months. Your voice matters. Policymakers need to hear not only about the legal and technical aspects of this initiative, but about its practical importance for businesses, investors and entrepreneurs.

Let me conclude with a simple point. Europe does not lack entrepreneurs, talent or innovative ideas. What we have lacked for too long is a genuinely European framework that allows those ideas to become successful European businesses. EU Inc. is our opportunity to change that. It is an opportunity to make the Single Market work better for the next generation of European companies – helping them to start here, scale here and succeed globally from a European base. We have made important progress. Now we must finish the job – and ensure that the ambition of this initiative is fully reflected in the final legislation.

Thank you!

So, the digital euro is not just an insurance policy against the dangers of dependency.

It provides the foundation for innovative European alternatives that can push every actor in the payments ecosystem to raise its game.

Turning to privacy.

The digital euro will provide cash-like privacy in the digital world for offline payments and a high degree of privacy for online payments.

In doing so, it strikes the right balance between citizens' legitimate request for privacy in payments and the need to counter money laundering, terrorist financing and fraud risks.

Let me state one important principle: for the digital euro to succeed – and most importantly for its legal tender status to be effective – we need that the online and offline features of the digital euro are implemented from the beginning.

This is in line with the European Parliament's vote on 10 February.

Payment service providers, merchants and consumers need clarity and predictability to plan for the issuance of digital euro.

To conclude, ladies and gentlemen,

At the beginning of my remarks, I referred to how the digital transition has changed every aspect of our lives.

That even extends to how we express ourselves.

I am sure that you are all familiar with the term FOMO, the fear of missing out.

Europe did not reap the full benefits of the first wave of the digital revolution.

We are still paying the price for this.

In fact, the productivity gap between the EU and the US is largely explained by the tech sector.

At this critical juncture, Europe cannot afford to miss out on the opportunities presented by the next wave of digitalisation.

The digital euro is an important part of this puzzle.

Making it a reality would send the clearest signal: Europe is ready to claim its place in the digital age.

Thank you.

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, 20 Jan 2026

As Europe becomes increasingly digital, we need to make sure our security is guaranteed also in the cyber domain.

We are in the middle of hybrid warfare. Every day, critical infrastructure in Europe is affected by cyber-attacks.

These operations span espionage, prepositioning, ransomware and disruptive operations.

They are often part of wider hybrid campaigns – combined with information operations or physical disruptions such as violations of our airspace or sabotages of critical infrastructure.

We have assessed our digital security environment and are proposing updates to our cybersecurity legislation to ensure that citizens, businesses, and our society remain secure. Specifically, we are today proposing four improvements:

First, by ensuring that we have a strong EU Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA;

Second, by ensuring that we have processes in place for derisking our ICT supply chain, while strengthening European critical infrastructure;

Third, by ensuring that we have a lean and efficient certification system that will make sure businesses operating in the EU market and our consumers can trust that they have access to products they use are security by design;

And last but not least, with the proposal for the Directive amending the NIS2 Directive - simplifying the compliance with our cybersecurity rules for businesses.

With today's proposal, we are making sure that EU Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA is equipped to carry out all new tasks reflecting a changing security environment.

ENISA will continue to support Member States in collaboration with national cybersecurity agencies to meet our common security challenges. ENISA will serve as:

A single entry point for incident reporting;

Producer of early alerts of cyber threats; and

Provide help-desk in cooperation with Europol and CSIRTs to support companies in responding and recovering from ransomware attacks.

ENISA will also be tasked to develop a common Union vulnerability management service capacity and provide vulnerability management services to stakeholders.

Moving on to the second part of our proposal. In the recently adopted Joint Communication on strengthening EU economic security, the Commission has highlighted a number of areas where dependencies on a single or a limited number of suppliers could pose a significant security risk.

We are turning the 5G Cybersecurity Toolbox into a mandatory approach to ensure a level-playing field and non-fragmentation of the EU market.

Together with Member States, we will identify which specific components of in the ICT supply chain of our critical sectors would require targeted mitigating measures. We propose a range of possible derisking measures, including restriction for high-risk suppliers.

A few weeks ago, we proposed key simplification measures for cybersecurity under the Digital Omnibus.

Our proposal included a single-entry point for incident notifications. With Cybersecurity Package, we are making it even easier to implement and comply with our rules. Notably, we propose targeted amendments to the NIS2 Directive to:

Clarify certain aspects regarding the scope and definitions. This will improve legal clarity and remove compliance burden for almost 30 000 companies, including over 6 000 micro- and small-sized enterprises;

We introduced a new category of small mid-cap enterprises that will reduce the compliance costs for over 22 000 companies;

Finally, we aim to add measures to streamline the collection of data on ransomware attacks.

When it comes to certification, we already have a system in place, which unfortunately has not produced the expected results.

We need a framework that is more dynamic, more straightforward and more efficient. ENISA will be managing the certification schemes and making sure that standards are as global as possible.

The renewed European Cybersecurity Certification Framework will unleash the potential of cybersecurity certification and contribute to ensuring that products and services are secure-by-design.

Used as a compliance tool, the certification will allow for a major simplification effort for EU companies, to more easily demonstrate across the whole internal market with requirements from the NIS 2 Directive.

In today's world, everything is digitised and all our daily lives are dependant on safe and functional information networks. Cybersecurity has gained greater importance and become an integral part of our comprehensive security.

We need to equip ourselves with robust and efficient cybersecurity tools that allow for a seamless cooperation across the EU. Because a cyber threat to one Member State is a threat to all.

Full text, original language (EN): Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection, 9 Dec 2025

Good afternoon, ladies and gentlemen,

Today was the final meeting of the EU Justice Ministers under the Danish Presidency. It has been a busy and successful 6-months to say the least.

So let me begin by thanking Peter - Minister Hummelgaard - for the Danish Presidency's commitment to an ambitious programme in the area of justice.

And today's programme was no exception; we covered GDPR simplification, model provisions for EU criminal law, the fight against impunity in the context of Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine, as well as the fight against drug trafficking and organised crime, access to data for effective criminal investigations, and extradition and judicial cooperation with third countries. I also updated ministers on the recent publication of our Digital justice and judicial training strategies, and the annual report on the application of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU. Despite this long list of discussion points, I will try to give you a brief summary of each.

Firstly, we discussed the way forward for a simplified application of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This follows a report from the Presidency, and the Commission's Digital Omnibus which we presented just last month. The Commission's proposed amendments to the GDPR are part of our wider aim to ensure that EU laws and policies are clearer and easier to understand, faster to implement, and so support a more competitive and prosperous European economy. These well-targeted amendments aim to harmonise , clarify , and simplify the application of the GDPR, while continuing to ensure a high-level of data protection across the EU. In our discussion, there was a strong general consensus that the core principles and requirements of the GDPR must be upheld.

Today, the Council approved its Conclusions on Model provisions for EU criminal law . These conclusions are part of our joint efforts to bring more coherence and consistency to EU criminal law. A priority echoed by stakeholders during our High-Level Forum on the Future of EU Criminal Justice. I reiterated that the Commission continues to stand ready to work with the co-legislators in shaping an even more coherent and consistent EU criminal justice system in the future.

As we approach four years into Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine, the Commission continues to work to ensure accountability for Russia's heinous crimes. I stressed the importance of the timely establishment of the Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression. The Commission is preparing a proposal on the signing of the Enlarged Partial Agreement of the Special Tribunal, with a view to becoming one of its Founding Members. Today, I encouraged Member States to do the same. And we have already pledged €10 million to support the swift establishment of the Special Tribunal through the Advance Team.

Today, I also reiterated the critical importance of the International Criminal Court in investigating and prosecuting crimes, and noted ways we might enhance technical cooperation between the ICC and EU agencies to support their work further. Justice for Ukraine and Ukrainians also means reparations for the damages caused.

I also highlighted the importance of the swift establishment of the International Claims Commission for Ukraine . The Commission has already tabled a proposal to sign the Convention establishing the Claims Commission which will be adopted by the Council soon. On 16 December, I will attend the Diplomatic Conference on the signature of the Convention establishing the Claims Commission. I encouraged all Member States to join me.

Today, I also thanked the Danish Presidency and previous Presidencies for their clear commitment to implementing the Roadmap to fight drug trafficking and organised crime . Dismantling international organised crime networks requires cooperation along the criminal justice chain – and this includes judicial cooperation to ensure that members can be arrested , prosecuted , and sentenced and assets can be seized .

Eurojust – the EU's Agency for criminal justice cooperation – has an essential role to play in this, as the Agency supports national authorities in their investigations and prosecutions – in close coordination with Europol . This is why – next year – the Commission will propose a revision of Eurojust's founding Regulation – to strengthen its mandate. The European Public Prosecutor's Office also has a crucially important role to play, due to financial crime also comprising an increasingly large part of organised crime networks' operations. This is why the Commission will ask the Council for mandates to negotiate agreements with third countries for cooperation with both Eurojust and – for the first time – also with the EPPO .

Closely related to these points was the discussion on how we can ensure that serious criminals fleeing the EU for third countries can still be brought to justice through extradition . As organised crime is more mobile , international , and sophisticated than ever, criminal groups exploit borders , legal gaps , and uneven levels of cooperation with third countries to escape justice. I reiterated the importance of multilateral agreements to that effect – that is, conventions of the Council of Europe and of the United Nations.

Further related to supporting these aims is the importance of ensuring access to data for effective criminal investigations. The Commission remains committed to ensuring effective access to data for law enforcement and judicial authorities in the context of criminal investigations and proceedings.

This afternoon, I was also pleased to present the recently adopted Digital Justice Package , which includes the DigitalJustice@2030 Strategy and the European Judicial Training Strategy 2025-2030 . The Digital Justice Strategy aims to accelerate the digitalisation of justice systems to increase their efficiency , leading to cost savings , making justice systems more resilient , and so improve access to justice for both citizens and business. The Judicial Training Strategy focuses on digitalisation , setting the ambition that by 2030 every justice professional in Europe will have the digital skills and knowledge of EU law required to operate confidently in the digital environment.

This afternoon, I also presented the Commission's Annual report on the application of the Charter of Fundamental Rights – adopted just last week. We are now half-way through the Commission's Strategy to strengthen the application of the Charter in the EU , which will run until 2030. This mid-term review takes stock of the past five years of implementation of the Charter strategy, setting out new measures to strengthen the implementation of the Charter by the Commission and the Member States. One of these is our shared responsibility to protect and promote fundamental rights – from European to national level. I encouraged Ministers to distribute the Charter report and use it to inform their work.

With this, I conclude my survey of today's varied and rich discussions.

Thank you for your attention. I look forward to answering any questions you may have.
