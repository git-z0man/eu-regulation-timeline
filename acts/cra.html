Public Procurement Act (PPA)

Regulation on public contracts and concessions, repealing Directives 2014/23/EU, 2014/24/EU and 2014/25/EU

COM(2026) 590 · proposed 9 Sep 2026 · read: EN · DE (en.md de.md) · Legislative procedure →

Why it is listed

Would delete Art. 5 CRA (procurement or use of products with digital elements) and move its rule into the Public Procurement Act: Art. 68 of the proposal makes Member States ensure that compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements of Annex I CRA is taken into account when such products are procured. References to Art. 5 CRA would be read as references to the PPA (Art. 147(11); the correlation table of the proposal names Art. 67). The CRA is one of 13 acts whose procurement rules the PPA consolidates (recital 63).

Article 147 – Amendments to horizontal public procurement provisions (Commission proposal, PPA)

  • (11) Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 is deleted.
  • References to Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 shall be construed as references to Article 68 of this Regulation;

4 Nov 2026ENISA Technical Advisory on Secure Update Mechanisms
Our summary of the draft of 29 May 2026: Draft of ENISA's Technical Advisory on Secure Update Mechanisms, version 0.1 of May 2026, put out by ENISA for public consultation through EUSurvey until 10 July 2026, according to a relay of the consultation; it is non-binding guidance, not final, and has since been replaced by the final version 1.0 of 4 November 2026. Addressed mainly to micro, small and medium-sized manufacturers of products with digital elements, it describes a seven-step update life cycle (build and sign, publish, check for updates, retrieve, verify, install, record status) and four delivery models, lists the threats at each step with real incidents, and recommends controls in four stages, among them signed update metadata, protected signing keys, untrusted distribution channels, anti-rollback checks, atomic installation with recovery, and security updates delivered separately from functional changes and installed automatically by default where feasible. It links these controls to the security update requirements of Annex I to the Cyber Resilience Act and states that it is no legal advice, no formal interpretation of that Regulation and no presumption of conformity.
Our summary of the final of 4 Nov 2026: Final version 1.0 of ENISA's Technical Advisory on Secure Update Mechanisms, published on 4 November 2026 after a public consultation in June and July 2026 that brought 12 contributions; it is non-binding guidance for manufacturers of products with digital elements, mainly small and medium-sized ones, and states that it is no legal advice, no formal interpretation of the Cyber Resilience Act and no presumption of conformity. Against the consultation draft of May 2026 it keeps the seven-step update life cycle, the four delivery models and the four stages of controls, and adds among other things that the recommendations are to be applied proportionately, secure boot, protection of verified updates until they are installed, key lifetimes and the migration of cryptographic algorithms, staged roll-out, machine-readable vulnerability status (VEX) and planning for the continuity of update services over the supported product lifetime. It cites the security update requirements of Annex I to the Cyber Resilience Act in the Regulation's own numbering, keeps the draft's recommendation of automatic security updates by default where feasible, with user controls to opt out or postpone, and now says that its thermostat example assumes a non-safety-critical residential deployment, not an industrial control or safety-regulated environment. ENISA says the contributions were addressed to the maximum extent possible, but the document does not say which change follows from which contribution.

17 Sep 2026Draft ENISA Technical Advisory on AI-assisted software development
Our summary: Draft of an ENISA Technical Advisory on AI-assisted software development, version 0.4 of September 2026, put out by ENISA for public consultation; it is non-binding guidance, not final, and says itself that it gives no legal guidance and introduces no new compliance framework. It describes the parts of AI-assisted development (models, coding assistants and agents, tools, project instructions), lists non-adversarial, adversarial and governance risks, and proposes four actions for each AI-assisted activity: identify the lifecycle process and the security expectations, specify them for the assistant or agent, verify the outputs through review and security checks, and record the evidence. It links this to the secure by design approach of the Cyber Resilience Act without interpreting any of its provisions, and its annex shows, with dependency selection as the example, how ENISA's advisory on package managers can be written into a reusable "skill" for a coding agent.

17 Dec 2025ENISA Technical Advisory for Secure Use of Package Managers
Our summary of the final of 10 Mar 2026: Final version (v1.1, March 2026) of ENISA's Technical Advisory for Secure Use of Package Managers, non-binding guidance for developers that ENISA released after a call for feedback from 17 December 2025 to 20 January 2026, which brought 15 contributions. Like the consultation draft, it explains how package managers such as npm, pip and Maven pull in direct and transitive dependencies, describes the risks of consuming third-party packages and recommends practices for selecting, integrating, monitoring and mitigating vulnerabilities in packages (among them an SBOM, lockfile and hash verification, trusted registries and an assessment of exploitability and reachability). Against the draft it adds that developers should first ask whether a dependency is needed at all, states that the tools named are only examples, points to machine-readable advisories (CSAF) and adds a section on AI-assisted development and "slopsquatting". Apart from ENISA's standard legal notice, which mentions the Cybersecurity Act (Regulation (EU) 2019/881), it names no legal act; it does not name the Cyber Resilience Act.
Our summary of the draft of 17 Dec 2025: Draft of ENISA's Technical Advisory for Secure Use of Package Managers (ENISA-TG-2025-01, version 0.8 of December 2025), published by ENISA for public consultation through a survey that closed in January 2026; it is non-binding guidance, and this draft has since been replaced by the final version of March 2026. It explains how package managers such as npm, pip and Maven pull in direct and transitive dependencies, describes the risks of consuming third-party packages (vulnerable or unmaintained packages, malicious and compromised packages, typosquatting, dependency confusion) and recommends practices for four stages: selecting packages, integrating them (SBOM, lockfiles and hash checks, trusted registries, restricted installation scripts), monitoring them and mitigating vulnerabilities after an assessment of their exploitability and reachability. Apart from ENISA's standard legal notice, which mentions the Cybersecurity Act (Regulation (EU) 2019/881), the draft names no legal act. It does not name the Cyber Resilience Act.

Reported vulnerabilities

Full statistics

Since 11 September 2026 manufacturers notify actively exploited vulnerabilities in their products through ENISA's single reporting platform (Art. 14 CRA, which applies from that day under Art. 71(2) CRA; the platform: Art. 16 CRA). Once a fix is available, ENISA adds a notified vulnerability to the EU Vulnerability Database (Art. 17(5) CRA). ENISA says that the notifications will also feed the EU catalogue of known exploited vulnerabilities (EU KEV).

What the figures cannot show. No public source records how a vulnerability reached ENISA: the EUVD has no field for the reporting route, and the notifications made through the single reporting platform are not public. The figures show what the database shows, not how many notifications manufacturers made. What the dates of the EU KEV do and do not show: note on the EU KEV.

1,826 vulnerabilities are marked as actively exploited in the EUVD; 92 are in the EU KEV catalogue, 37 of them added since the platform went live on 11 Sep 2026. Data as of 27 Nov 2026.

Horizontal standards (EN 40000-1 series, CEN/CLC/JTC 13)

The horizontal standards in support of the CRA: vocabulary, principles and risk management, vulnerability handling, and the generic security requirements of Annex I, Part I, point (2) CRA. Only the standard for the vulnerability handling requirements of Annex I, Part II (EN 40000-1-3) is intended for citation in the Official Journal and can thus give a presumption of conformity (Art. 27(1) CRA). The standards for the requirements of Annex I, Part I (EN 40000-1-2, the future EN 40000-1-4) and the vocabulary are harmonised standards requested by M/606 but not intended for citation: the CEN-CENELEC project pages say “No citation expected”, and they have no Annex ZA. Manufacturers can apply them, but they give no presumption of conformity.

Steps (Start NWI · Drafting prEN · Voting prEN · Drafting EN · Voting EN · Adoption EN · HAS process · Citation hEN): the step a project has reached, after the status on its public CEN-CENELEC or ETSI page (as in DKE's overview of the CRA projects): filled = done, arrow = current, hatched = no citation expected for this act. A published standard is in the HAS process until it is cited in the Official Journal. Our reading of the public status, not an official stage.

Text expected: the quarter in which the CEN-CENELEC project plan expects the definitive text (DAV), the step before the Commission can cite a harmonised standard in the Official Journal. Rounded; a forecast of the technical body, not a commitment. Plan outdated: the plan's next step is more than 90 days overdue, so its dates do not reflect the current progress.

Annex Z: whether the draft must contain an Annex Z(A) that relates its clauses to the essential requirements; without it, a standard cannot give a presumption of conformity. Citation: whether the standard is a candidate for citation in the Official Journal. Commission assessment: the result of the Commission's latest assessment of the draft against the standardisation request (phase: ENQ enquiry, FV formal vote). All three as recorded by CEN-CENELEC for the project; they can change until the standard is cited.

Contradictory: the citation status of the project plan and the public CEN-CENELEC project page disagree; both are shown, neither is preferred.

  • 2026, Action 5: Cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements
    Legal reference
    Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements (Cyber Resilience Act)
    Planned deliverables
    Standardisation request to develop harmonised European standards in support of Regulation (EU) 2024/8247 (Cyber Resilience Act), complementing Commission Implementing Decision C(2025) 618, standardisation request M/606. While M/606 mandates horizontal cybersecurity properties and vertical standards for Annex III/IV critical products, this request addresses gaps for products with digital elements outside those annexes, notably in rail, machinery, lifts, agricultural machinery, artificial intelligence systems and solar inverters. Deliverables will implement the Act’s essential requirements in these domains, including test methods and conformity evidence. Standards will align with Union legislation, delimit scope to avoid overlap, and facilitate publication of references in the Official Journal of the EU.
    Objective
    Enable secure products with digital elements outside CRA Annexes III–IV through harmonised standards that confer presumption of conformity with Annex I and support lifecycle cybersecurity obligations.

Madam President, honourable Members and Executive Vice-President of the Commission, cyber resilience of critical infrastructure and supply chains is becoming more and more essential, as digitalisation impacts all sectors, and as the threats to our security evolve.

Indeed, that is something every one of us here today, in this Parliament and across the EU, are concerned with, particularly due to recent, and perhaps not so recent, events.

Back in May, this Parliament debated advanced AI systems and how they can be used to combat cyberattacks. Now we see attacks on our supermarkets that threaten our supply chains and impact the everyday lives of our citizens.

Indeed, just today and in previous days, we see threats issued to schools in my own country, Ireland, and in other EU Member States. These threats are designed to cause real anxiety, huge uncertainty and fear among parents, school communities and, indeed, our population in general.

So our challenge as a European Union is that we need to be prepared and, where at all possible, anticipate such threats.

Today's debate is very timely because it shows once more the importance of maintaining our efforts to continuously strengthen our cybersecurity. That is why, at European level, our institutions have passed landmark legislations during the previous parliamentary term – in particular, the NIS2 Directive and the Cyber Resilience Act. The implementation of these instruments is essential to further increase our collective capacity to protect our citizens and our economies against a multiplication of malicious actors.

As the Irish Presidency, we are fully committed to advancing negotiations on the cybersecurity package, covering both the revision of the Cybersecurity Act and the Amending NIS2 Directive.

In that context, I can say we have made real progress on the three main parts of the Cybersecurity Act. That is, first of all, the strengthening of ENISA. Secondly, making certification frameworks more efficient and effective. And thirdly, improving protection of our critical ICT supply chains.

So, can I conclude by saying, we still aim to reach agreement in the Council before the end of our presidency, so that our Lithuanian colleagues can start the trilogue when the Parliament is ready.

We are convinced that this legislation will significantly contribute to the strengthening of our cybersecurity, because preventing successful attacks, by better protecting our networks and the whole ICT supply system, is key.

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, for the Commission

Madam President, honourable Members, Council, cybersecurity is very essential part of European preparedness and defence. Cyber resilience is not only about preventing attacks, but about ensuring that our societies, our economies and our defence capabilities can continue to function during crises.

The risk of disruption to the Union's critical infrastructure by foreign actors is real. Just last week, we saw refrigeration systems in supermarkets and blood banks throughout Europe switched off because of cyber incidents, and the estimated cost was in the tens and hundreds of millions. But it is not only about the financial impact, it's also about the societal impact. Although the attacks did not cause any significant impact on food security and public health, it should be a wake-up call for everybody: Europe cannot afford to have dependencies or strategic weaknesses that expose our critical infrastructure to unacceptable risks. For instance, we cannot allow risks of foreign interference, espionage or disruption in our most critical ICT supply chains.

And this is particularly relevant in our communications infrastructure, such as our 5G networks. They are increasingly relevant for reliable ultrafast connectivity, data and information sharing, drone detection and also real-time operational coordination. And this is why supply chain security is at the core of the proposal for the revision of the Cybersecurity Act, which is now on the table in this House.

We need a strong, pragmatic and proportionate approach to de-risking our essential ICT supply chains, and we need to act now to de-risk our 5G networks. It has been almost seven years since the EU adopted the 5G cybersecurity toolbox. The time for hesitation is over. We need to be much more decisive and ambitious, including when it comes to setting clear timelines for phasing out high risk suppliers. If we fail to act decisively now, we are not only putting our 5G networks at risk, but we will also carry these dependencies into 6G, the next generation of connectivity.

A strong and cyber-resilient Europe, also needs a strong European cybersecurity agency, where the resources match the mandate and the challenges. In the Commission's action plan on cybersecurity and AI, ENISA is playing an important role by strengthening the resilience of our critical and other vital infrastructure against AI-driven cyberattacks, and by testing and assessing the cybersecurity capabilities of frontier AI models.

All this clearly demonstrates the potential of ENISA. This is also a very concrete example of European solidarity and cooperation. Through ENISA, we can ensure that all our 27 Member States benefit from shared expertise, knowledge and capabilities rather than each having to develop them independently. And this is why I urge the European Parliament to support our efforts to equip ENISA with the mandate, resources and capabilities it needs to support our Member States and protect Europe's critical infrastructure, not only against the threats we face today, but also against those that are emerging on the horizon.

Honourable Members, in recent months, we have witnessed a wave of high-profile cyber incidents and other hybrid attacks, from attacks on public administrations and personal data breaches to attacks on health, telecommunications and energy infrastructure, including subsea cables and, as I mentioned earlier, on supermarkets and blood banks.

These incidents underline the importance of the regulatory framework we have put in place at European level. We can already see the difference that the Cyber Resilience Act will make by strengthening the security of digital products placed on the EU market and in many cases, industrial devices such as refrigeration systems are unnecessarily exposed to the internet, or they are configured with weak passwords or run on outdated software. And these are low-hanging fruit, because many of these incidents therefore demonstrate the importance of the basic cybersecurity requirements set out in the NIS2 Directive. Had these requirements been fully transposed and effectively implemented, some of these incidents could potentially have been prevented or their impact significantly reduced. The challenge before us is therefore not only to develop new policies and new instruments, but to ensure that the rules we have already agreed are translated into real security on the ground.

Because ultimately, cybersecurity is not only about legislation about technology, it is about protecting our economies, safeguarding the essential services on which our citizens depend, and ensuring that Europe has the resilience and capacity to act in an increasingly uncertain world. And that is the responsibility we must now deliver on together.

Mr President, honourable Members, today, the Commission has adopted a new action plan on cybersecurity and artificial intelligence. It follows a commitment I made to this House in May, in light of the unprecedented cyber security capabilities of the latest models. Such models can be used not only for defensive, but also for offensive objectives in cyberspace. Their new capabilities are marking a strategic shift for the Union's security. Most importantly, these models fundamentally now change the economics of malicious actors, whether cyber criminals or national states. These advanced AI models can now build cyber exploits in minutes or hours at a fraction of the cost of vulnerability discovery by trained humans. Once weaponised, these vulnerabilities endanger the security of our infrastructure and society, so we must be prepared for these new capabilities being available widely, including to malicious actors, especially as other capabilities, (inaudible), for instance, are also growing.

The Commission's response has been immediate. We engaged with the entire AI and cybersecurity ecosystem, with you, with Member States, with our AI providers, with industry, and with our international partners. The action plan is not starting from scratch, as we know. The EU already has very robust legal and policy foundations. We are now turning them into action: capability and resilience along three priorities.

So first, AI models with high cyber capabilities deployed in Europe must be safe and our European players must have access to use them. The AI Act provides us with the world's most advanced framework to protect us against the risks of AI. And in less than one month, our AI office will also have the enforcement powers to ensure that providers also establish appropriate safeguards against misuse, including in the cyber domain. Assessing models before release is a cornerstone of risk mitigation. Europe must build excellence in this area. We will launch a call to step up Europe's evaluation capacity of AI models, including in cybersecurity, by 2027. The EU agency for cybersecurity, ENISA, will play an important role in making this action plan operational and together with ENISA, the Commission will also develop a European blueprint for structural access to advanced AI models with a focus on cybersecurity. We need common, clear and also transparent conditions for accessing these models, which also serves as a basis for cooperation and alignment with our international partners. And we must also strengthen our know-how on how to deploy these technologies for our cybersecurity. And this is why already by the end of this year, the Commission will also set up a secure platform to test how these models can be used safely for cybersecurity in critical sectors such as finance, energy, health, transport or public administration.

Second, we must protect ourselves against malicious attacks exploiting such models. Our immediate priority is now to identify and fix the most critical vulnerabilities, faster. The first step is the full and effective implementation of our cybersecurity legislation, so the NIS2 Directive, DORA and the Cyber Resilience Act. Not tomorrow, now. And to fix these vulnerabilities where exposure is the highest for our critical infrastructure, this is open source software. So we will launch a critical open source resilience campaign. In parallel, we will also support our critical sectors and our SMEs in understanding and also deploying existing AI tools, including those openly available to enhance their cyber resilience.

Third, we must build Europe's own AI powered cyber capabilities. We cannot rely only on non-European solutions for capabilities that are becoming critical for our security. So Europe has everything it takes to be competitive in this area. We have talent, a solid cybersecurity and AI ecosystem, and with AI factories and in the future also AI gigafactories, we have also the infrastructure. But we must get serious about financing. Frontier AI requires very large-scale investment, as we know. Far beyond what public funding alone can provide. We need to mobilise private capital especially equity. The new European tech equity capacity announced in the tech sovereignty package can be a game-changer here. Some will say that the cost of building European frontier capabilities is too high. In today's world, the cost of not building them will be much higher and it will grow every year. So our action plan now addresses the pressing challenges that frontier AI models present to cybersecurity, and we are building capacities for a stronger Europe in this domain.

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, for the Commission

Madam President, honourable Members, thank you for this opportunity, for this very timely debate. Artificial intelligence can be used for good and for bad, and this applies also in cybersecurity. AI can equip cyber-defenders with powerful tools to protect our critical infrastructure. At the same time, it poses grave risks when exploited by malicious actors, and this has now expanded to a new level and pace.

These new frontier AI models possess high capabilities. They operate at a speed and level of automation that outpaces traditional defensive cycles, at the same time also lowering significantly the barrier to entry for sophisticated attacks.

The new concern is that vulnerabilities can now be identified at an unprecedented speed, reducing what would normally take months to hours, outpacing any human expertise in this field. We cannot be certain that this will be the same for fixing these vulnerabilities, and this is the risk we need to be prepared to address.

In recent weeks, we have had the launch of such models for cybersecurity by two of the most advanced AI players from the US, and it is clear that we will see more and more that kind of model entering the market also from other parts of the world.

In the face of these challenges to cybersecurity, we need to do two things. First, we need to make sure that the EU's robust legal framework is applied. And, second, see if there are remaining issues that need to be addressed.

First, under our AI Act, providers of the most advanced AI models must notify the Commission when they know they have such a model. They must also assess and mitigate systemic risks, including those arising from potential cyber‑misuse.

Second, the NIS 2 Directive requires critical infrastructure operators to take state‑of‑the‑art measures to counter all cybersecurity risks, including those stemming from the malicious use of frontier AI.

Third, the Cyber Resilience Act mandates manufacturers of hardware and software to test their products for vulnerabilities before placing them on the market and throughout their lifecycle. It also requires timely fixing of any vulnerabilities discovered. The emergence of frontier AI models makes the swift transportation and implementation of this legal framework a key priority.

The Commission will ensure that these frameworks are implemented as quickly as possible, and we will also make sure that their implementation fully addresses both the risks and the potential of AI. We have also in place a cyber‑emergency mechanism which can support preparedness testing and scanning, and we will see how to deploy the EU Cyber‑Reserve for the purpose of preparedness as a matter of priority. We are working very closely with ENISA, our EU agency for cybersecurity, and national cybersecurity authorities in the Member States on this matter.

We have not waited for these models to appear to anticipate future cybersecurity capabilities. As part of the upcoming technological sovereignty package, we will focus on ensuring the maintenance, security and integrity of our open source digital infrastructures that are coming under increased cybersecurity pressure resulting from the weaponisation of AI. Our proposal for a revised Cybersecurity Act is designed precisely to equip ENISA and the Commission with the resources, powers, and clarity of mandate that this new threat picture is requiring.

ENISA is still a very small agency, compared both with the national cybersecurity agencies, but also with other security‑related Union agencies. We are also now proposing measures to ensure that information flows faster to those who need to act on it, through early alerts, and also collaboration on vulnerabilities.

To ramp up our preparedness in the face of emerging AI threats, I will also present a list of actions in the coming weeks, bringing together the best EU expertise in AI and cybersecurity, and in doing so, we will work with like‑minded partners.

Should more need to be done, we are certainly open to considering this, and this is where the European Parliament can play a very decisive role in the negotiations on the Cybersecurity Act, which we presented last January. Our proposal is a horizontal framework where we can anticipate these issues and where we need to give the EU Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, the powers and the scope of the mandate they need to help our Member States face these challenges.

So we count on the European Parliament's support in this respect. The Commission has been engaging closely with the providers of the recently launched advanced AI models from the very start to better understand and also anticipate their cybersecurity capabilities and how these companies plan to address associated risks.

In the current geopolitical context, where even the smallest vulnerability in our ICT supply chains can be exploited by malicious actors, AI‑powered cyber‑defences are essential to safeguarding our critical infrastructure. An essential first step in this is the rapid adoption of already‑available advanced cyber‑tools by public and private European organisations for their own cyber‑defence.

There is no reason to wait, and this is crucial for preparing for the upcoming risks, but we do not want to remain dependent on others' cyber‑defence tools. We must do more to nurture our European cybersecurity ecosystem. We have already European cybersecurity companies who boast impressive vulnerability discovery capabilities on par with the big US models. But these companies are still very small, and this is why Europe must invest in our own frontier AI and cybersecurity capabilities.

We need to encourage and support European companies to develop advanced AI models, including open‑weight models, so that in Europe we have the right conditions for sustainable cybersecurity business models to meet the challenges of today and tomorrow.

Advanced AI models create many possibilities. They can help us to prepare and protect but they can also be used against us.

Advanced AI can create cyber exploits in just minutes or hours, for a fraction of the cost of human experts. While a major vulnerability can cost over 1 million EUR on the grey market, AI only needs data center compute power. Once weaponised, these flaws can endanger our infrastructure and society, which is built upon digital systems.

The Commission's response has been immediate. We engaged with the entire AI and cybersecurity ecosystem: with Member States, with AI providers, with industry, and with our international partners.

We are not starting from scratch. The EU already has robust legal and policy foundations to address these challenges. We are now turning them into action, capability, and resilience, along three priorities.

First, AI models with high cyber capabilities must be safe. And Europeans must have access to use them.

Through the AI Act, the EU has the world's strongest framework to protect us from the potential risks of advanced AI. The Commission's AI Office is already working with AI companies to implement these rules.

Under the AI Act, advanced AI models must be evaluated, and mitigation measures carefully assessed, before the models are placed on the EU market.

In less than a month, the AI Office will have enforcement powers. Testing models before they are released is key to reducing risk. To build expertise, the EU will launch a call to boost its AI evaluation capacity—especially in cybersecurity—aiming to be operational by 2027.

The EU Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, will play an important role in operationalising this Action Plan, as well as some Member States.

Together with ENISA, we will develop a European Blueprint for structured access to advanced AI models, with focus on cybersecurity.

The Blueprint will support both AI providers and European organisations, including companies, on how to identify those who get access and on which terms. It will also serve as a basis for cooperation and alignment with international partners.

But access is not enough. We must also strengthen our know-how on how to deploy these technologies for our cybersecurity, by testing their cyber capabilities.

This is why the Commission will set up a secure platform to test AI for cybersecurity already by the end of 2026.

The platform will allow us to assess how models can be used safely for cybersecurity operations in critical sectors such as finance, energy, health, transport or public administration.

Second, we must also be able to protect ourselves against malicious attacks stemming from the misuse of such models.

Our immediate priority is clear: identify and fix the most critical vulnerabilities faster. The first step is the full and effective implementation of our cybersecurity legislation: the NIS2 Directive, DORA, and the Cyber Resilience Act.

This applies to all member states – I want to take the opportunity to highlight the urgency of implementing especially the NIS2 directive.

But we also need targeted action where exposure is the highest for our critical infrastructure. We will therefore launch a Critical Open Source Resilience Campaign, to support maintainers in fixing the most critical vulnerabilities in key open-source projects.

AI is not only a risk: it is also a strategic enabler of cyber resilience. Existing AI tools can already help with vulnerability management, threat detection, and incident response. We must support their safe and secure uptake across critical sectors, SMEs and public authorities. And we must do so fast.

Third, we must build Europe's own AI-powered cyber capabilities.

We cannot rely only on non-European solutions for capabilities that are critical for our security.

Building up our own AI frontier models is costly, but in today's world, the cost of not building them will be even higher.

Frontier AI requires very large-scale investment, far beyond what public funding alone can provide. We need to mobilise private capital, and especially equity. The new European tech equity capacity announced in the Tech Sovereignty Package can be a game changer here.

Europe has what it takes to compete. We have the talent. We have a solid cybersecurity and AI ecosystem. We have AI Factories and, in the future, AI Gigafactories that can be part of a sovereign European infrastructure for AI and cybersecurity.

Full text, original language (EN): Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection, 17 Jun 2026

Mr President, dear Deputy Minister, dear honourable Members, today the threats we face are not isolated incidents, but coordinated waves of hybrid attacks aimed at weakening the very fabric of our democracies. This is why cases such as the Lithuanian case of the data breach that you put on the agenda of the Plenary today must be treated with the utmost seriousness.

While the specifics of the Lithuanian case would need to be addressed by the competent authorities, the scale of such a breach can generate distrust in the ability of democratic authorities and frameworks to safeguard the fundamental rights of our citizens.

The only durable and effective way forward against threats and challenges like these is to take a whole of government, a whole of society, and a whole of cycle approach to protect our collective democracies.

As part of this approach, the Commission adopted the European Democracy Shield in November of last year to strengthen and to protect our democracies. Its actions complement the broader EU work to counter hybrid threats.

In particular, we have now set up the European Centre for Democratic Resilience as a hub for strategic and practical cooperation between EU Member States and EU institutions and bodies, supporting the sharing of information, best practices and capacity-building. It can also serve as a nexus for facilitating practical cooperation and enabling the Member States to develop common response options when faced with similar challenges.

When we talk about hybrid threats, a key component is indeed cybersecurity. The NIS2 Directive ensures that our essential services know what measures to take in order to work on preventing cyber incidents.

Additionally, once it enters into application next year, the Cyber Resilience Act will make sure digital products are secure all across the EU. In addition, the Cyber Solidarity Act establishes a European Cybersecurity Reserve, a pool of trusted experts who can be called upon to support any country that may be in need.

We should also make use of the EU hybrid toolbox and its associated cyber diplomacy and FIMI toolboxes, which give us the means to identify those responsible for malicious hybrid activities and to respond through coordinated diplomatic action.

We have also proposed a revision of the Cybersecurity Act to strengthen the mandate of ENISA, equip it with the right resources, and to address risks across ICT supply chains.

Democratic resilience also depends on the enforcement of rules in reality, including the application of the General Data Protection Regulation. The GDPR places clear obligations on controllers to secure personal data through appropriate technical and organisational measures, and to notify national data protection authorities and affected individuals when breaches pose a high risk. Enforcement is then a matter for national authorities, who must investigate and follow up accordingly.

I look forward to hearing your contributions to today's debate and indeed to responding to them.
