MDR/IVDR simplification

Regulation amending Regulations (EU) 2017/745 and (EU) 2017/746 as regards simplifying and reducing the burden of the rules on medical devices and in vitro diagnostic medical devices

COM(2025) 1023 · proposed 16 Dec 2025 · Legislative procedure →

Why it is listed

Would move the Medical Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/745 and the IVD Regulation (EU) 2017/746 from Section A to Section B of Annex I AI Act (Art. 4 of the proposal). For products under Section B, only Art. 6(1), Art. 60a and Art. 102 to 112 AI Act apply (Art. 2(2) AI Act as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI); the high-risk requirements would be taken into account through the medical devices rules.

Digital Omnibus on AI

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230 as regards the simplification of the implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Digital Omnibus on AI)

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 · published 24 Jul 2026 · Legislative procedure →

Why it is listed

Amends the AI Act in 43 points to simplify its implementation; among others it replaces Art. 4 (AI literacy), moves the obligations for high-risk AI systems to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I) (Art. 113 as amended) and gives providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to comply with Art. 50(2) (Art. 111(4)).

Steps (Start NWI · Drafting prEN · Voting prEN · Drafting EN · Voting EN · Adoption EN · HAS process · Citation hEN): the step a project has reached, after the status on its public CEN-CENELEC or ETSI page (as in DKE's overview of the CRA projects): filled = done, arrow = current, hatched = no citation expected for this act. A published standard is in the HAS process until it is cited in the Official Journal. Our reading of the public status, not an official stage.

Text expected: the quarter in which the CEN-CENELEC project plan expects the definitive text (DAV), the step before the Commission can cite a harmonised standard in the Official Journal. Rounded; a forecast of the technical body, not a commitment. Plan outdated: the plan's next step is more than 90 days overdue, so its dates do not reflect the current progress.

Annex Z: whether the draft must contain an Annex Z(A) that relates its clauses to the essential requirements; without it, a standard cannot give a presumption of conformity. Citation: whether the standard is a candidate for citation in the Official Journal. Commission assessment: the result of the Commission's latest assessment of the draft against the standardisation request (phase: ENQ enquiry, FV formal vote). All three as recorded by CEN-CENELEC for the project; they can change until the standard is cited.

  • 2026, Action 1: Artificial intelligence
    Legal reference
    Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act)
    Planned deliverables
    Preparation of standardisation request to develop European standards and/or European standardisation deliverables in support of Article 40 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), covering: (i) reporting and documentation processes to improve the resource performance of artificial intelligence systems (including high-risk systems), with lifecycle measurement methods, metrics, boundary conditions and reporting templates for energy and other resource consumption; and (ii) deliverables on the energy-efficient development of general-purpose AI models (development, training and inference). The request complements, without duplicating, work already mandated by Commission Implementing Decision C(2025) 3871, standardisation request M/613. It excludes essential requirements for high-risk AI systems covered by M/613. Outputs will build on ongoing environmental-impact work and inform the Commission’s review under Article 112(6).
    Objective
    Promote human-centric, trustworthy AI that protects health, safety, fundamental rights, democracy, the rule of law and the environment, while limiting harmful effects and supporting innovation.

    2025, Action 11: Artificial intelligence

11 Nov 2026 · Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy

Brussels · Remarks by Executive Vice-President Virkkunen and Commissioner McGrath on the Digital Fairness Act and a Consumer enforcement initiative

Our summary: Virkkunen and McGrath present the Digital Fairness Act and a consumer enforcement initiative. The AI Act comes up once, in Virkkunen's list of digital rules the Commission enforces (with the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act); the speech says nothing about its content or its amendment.

We are delivering to that standard through the enforcement of the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act, and the AI Act. – Henna Virkkunen, original language (EN)

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, 11 Nov 2026

Executive Vice-President Virkkunen

Good morning, and welcome to the college readout. Today we present the Consumer Package.

This package has two proposals: the Digital Fairness Act (DFA) to simplify and modernise consumer protection for the digital age.

And the revision of Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) Regulation, to make enforcement of EU consumer law more effective and coherent.

In recent years, the European Union has set the global standard for a safe, secure and fairer online world. For citizens and businesses alike.

We are delivering to that standard through the enforcement of the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act, and the AI Act.

More recently, we have also proposed EU KIDS Act to ensure safety from the very beginning to our children. Once more Europe is showing leadership in protecting our users in the digital world.

Now, it was time to update consumer protection rules to ensure they remain a strong safety net in the digital world.

We are tackling the new unfair commercial practices that distort consumers' choices and put pressure on them, undermining their ability to make informed choices.

A clear example is manipulative design practices, so-called 'dark patterns'. When shopping online, we have all experienced fake urgency tactics, such as claims that “only 5 items remain”.

We are also empowering consumers: by giving them further control over addictive digital features, such as endless scrolling. Or by making subscriptions and digital contracts easier to understand and easier to cancel.

We are making influencer marketing and personalised commercial practices more transparent.

This is good for consumers, and it is also good for business. It creates a level playing field across the EU, just like we aim to do in other areas of our legislation.

In addition, the Commission is proposing to revise the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation.

We are giving the Commission direct investigation and sanctioning powers in very large-scale cases with a major impact on consumers across the Single Market.

This would include powers to impose fines of up to 6% of a trader's worldwide annual turnover, to improve coordination of cross-border enforcement actions by national authorities, and to strengthen enforcement against non-EU traders.

This coordination for cross-border actions is built into the design laws already when it comes to consumers. The Digital Fairness act will not duplicate this – it will reinforce our rulebook.

Lastly, the Commission is proposing targeted amendments to the Consumer Rights Directive to simplify its application in the digital space. This includes streamlining upfront information requirements for repeat in-app purchases and clarifying how consumers can cancel digital media subscriptions.

Most companies want to compete fairly and operate with legal certainty, without being undercut by those using manipulative tactics. By updating consumer law in a targeted, proportionate way, we are strengthening trust and providing businesses with clearer, more consistent rules across the Single Market.

Our message is simple: Europe already has a strong digital foundation. With the new Digital Fairness Act and the revised Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation, we update our safety net consumer rules for the digital age.

Commissioner McGrath

As the Executive Vice-President has just laid out, the Digital Fairness Act, together with the update to the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation, represents the most significant reform of EU consumer protection rules in more than 20 years.

The digital economy has transformed how we shop, consume entertainment and access information.

Our existing consumer laws were developed before the rise of e-commerce, before the attention economy and before the sophisticated use of personal data became central to how businesses reach and persuade consumers.

So, today's update builds on strong foundations of EU consumer law and our digital rulebook.

Our ambition is clear: more choice and control for consumers, simpler rules and fairer competition for businesses, and stronger enforcement to tackle infringements that cross borders.

When shopping online, consumers are being faced with practices designed to mislead, manipulate and exploit them.

They book a hotel or concert ticket – only to discover that the headline price does not tell the whole story, with hidden fees added along the way.

They sign up for a subscription – but find it deliberately difficult to cancel.

They follow influencers – without always realising what looks like a friendly recommendation is actually paid advertising.

And these techniques come at a financial cost: consumers are losing €10.3 billion per year.

This is happening at a time when households are already feeling the pressure of the cost of living.

Every euro matters. Consumers deserve to know what they are paying for - and to be in control of their choices.

The Digital Fairness Act will make sure the digital economy works for consumers, not against them.

The DFA will:

Prohibit manipulative design practices – such as dark patterns – that push consumers into making decisions against their own interests. Fake claims that a product is almost sold out will no longer have a place online;

It will give consumers the choice to turn off addictive features, such as endless scrolling and autoplay of videos;

Require influencers to disclose advertising and prohibit them from exploiting the trust of children;

Prohibit unfair personalisation practices such as advertising or pricing that targets minors or exploits vulnerabilities;

Ensure transparent pricing – including in the event ticket sector; when consumers wait in virtual queues, companies will have to show the current price in real time and the highest possible price;

The DFA will make digital contracts and subscriptions easier to cancel; consumers will now receive a reminder before a free trial is converted into a paid subscription, and an automatic renewal will be a conscious choice, not a forgotten obligation;

It will restrict unfair practices that encourage overspending, with children no longer be exposed to gambling-simulating loot boxes or mechanisms that pressure them to pay to progress in video games; and

When consumers really need help, they will be able to get meaningful assistance and reach a human being in customer service - not be left with a chatbot.

But today's package isn't just for consumers; it is about backing responsible businesses. Businesses that play by the rules should not be undercut by competitors who cut corners.

Our consumer protection rules will help ensure that businesses compete on the merits of their products and services - not on their ability to exploit consumers or evade the rules.

We are not making it harder to do business.

We are making it harder to do business unfairly.

But consumer rights are worth little if they cannot be enforced.

Today, when illegal commercial practices impact consumers across several Member States, cooperation between national authorities can be time consuming and ineffective.

This is why we are also proposing a revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation – fulfilling another priority initiative in the ‘One Europe, One Market' Roadmap.

The new enforcement reform will:

Speed-up cooperation between these authorities, setting timelines for key steps and improving how penalties are coordinated;

Give the Commission direct powers to investigate and issue fines in large-scale cases with a major impact on consumers across the Single Market, with fines of up to 6% of a company's worldwide annual turnover; and

Strengthen enforcement against non-EU companies, by requiring them to appoint a legal representative in the EU.

Europe has built one of the strongest consumer protection frameworks in the world. But our rules cannot stand still while the world around us changes.

We cannot have 21st-century consumers protected by rules that have not kept pace with the 21st-century economy.

Our ambition is straightforward: whether consumers are shopping on high streets or shopping online, they deserve the same protection, the same transparency and the same ability to make informed choices.

And this is not about putting consumers and businesses on opposite sides.

We want a digital economy where consumers can trust the services they use and where responsible businesses can compete and innovate.

Honourable Members, Ladies and Gentlemen,

Artificial Intelligence – the electricity of the mind; invisible, always on, and quietly threading through everything we do. The exponential rise of generative AI promises to produce the most seismic revolution in living memory. Its potential unparalleled , its trajectory unknown . And in my current position as European Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection, I see its revolutionary nature every day.

AI can help consumers save time and money, by comparing prices and detecting scams. AI can assist courts with document analysis and case management, reducing backlogs and delays while leaving decisions firmly in human hands. AI helps detect disinformation and foreign information manipulation and interference - supporting free and fair elections. Used responsibly, AI is not about replacing human judgment - it is about strengthening it, making our institutions fairer, our economies more competitive, and our democracies more resilient.

Yet we live in a world where we can't always rely on transformative tools like AI to be used responsibly. We must ensure we govern AI wisely – meaning innovation serves the public good, protects democratic values, and leaves no one behind. Based on 2023 data, 2 in 5 Europeans see false or misleading information circulating online and offline as the most serious threat to our democracies. And, since then, the integrity of our information space has come under increased threat by actors attempting to spread disinformation , manipulate public debate, and interfere in elections. Generative AI has contributed to these new techniques, and has transformed the landscape of influence. The EU's Digital Rulebook, which is there to ensure the accountability of service providers and their responsibility to protect users includes: the General Data Protection Regulation, the AI Act, the Digital Services Act and Code of Conduct on Disinformation, and the Political Advertising Regulation and the European Media Freedom Act. But what does this mean for citizens, for consumers, and for all of us sitting here today?

The GDPR puts citizens back in control of their personal data, ensuring that privacy is a right, not a privilege. The DSA makes online platforms accountable, so what is illegal offline is illegal online. It protects citizens from harmful content and disinformation, while safeguarding freedom of expression. The AI Act ensures AI serves people - not the other way around. The European Media Freedom Act protects independent journalism, and ensures citizens can access reliable information.

Beginning with the EU's AI Act; this act aims to strengthen our democracies by ensuring artificial intelligence is used transparently, responsibly, and in line with European values - protecting fundamental rights while building trust in the technologies that increasingly shape public life.

Entering into force in August 2024, it specifically prohibits certain practices, including AI-enabled manipulative or deceptive techniques , and AI systems that exploit vulnerabilities. These may include: AI systems generating particularly harmful deepfakes ; AI chatbots used to disseminate hateful content ; and systems intentionally designed to manipulate public opinion .

The obligations under the AI Act include: the duty to mark and enable detection when content has been artificially generated or manipulated , and to label deep fakes and certain AI-generated publications on public matters. But we need to continue to look closely at how and where AI is deployed. For example, the Commission is currently working on Guidelines on the classification of high-risk AI systems that cover electoral processes.

In all, the EU's citizen-centric regulatory approach towards AI aims to ensure that we can harness the AI revolution, all while protecting the integrity of Europe's information space, and so the resilience of its democracies.

They are here to stay, and our responsibility is not to wish them away, but to govern them wisely. The revision lays down a predictable framework for conformity assessments with c lear timelines and fees, kicking in already in the short-term . In 105 days, products should be certified now. This will be quicker in the US or China.

It reduces the reporting obligations, to what really matters.

This will particularly come to the benefit of SMEs, which make up 90% of the sector, and will give Europeans quicker access to life-saving technologies.

The revision will free at least 3 billion euros up for our companies to spend on innovation, and this is a conservative figure.

Safe Hearts Plan, our first-ever Response to cardiovascular disease.

Every year, more than 1.7 million Europeans die from cardiovascular diseases. Many of the underlying risk factors are rising: smoking, vaping, diabetes and obesity, including - and this crucial – particularly among young people. Genetic and hereditary risk factors

Without action, we face our whole next generation to be sick with a chronic disease, with all its economic and social consequences for us all. The Safe Hearts Plan is our answer to this huge societal challenge.

Nearly 80% of cardiovascular diseases can be avoided through prevention and lifestyle changes.

This is why Safe Hearts is strong on prevention, next to detection, treatment and care. It will promote regular checks and a common approach for Member States when developing national check-up programmes, alongside a European-wide “Know Your Numbers” campaign and a mobile screening pilot to increase participation.

We will also modernise the rules on tobacco control in 2026. In coming years, we will further address the issues around ultra-processed food.

Digital tools, big data and AI are transforming what prevention and early detection can look like. The new medical device reform will hugely support this.

The EU will also invest in 2026 €20 million in AI- and data-driven tools that enable earlier diagnosis, better risk prediction and more personalised prevention.

This Health Package is about safeguarding our unique healthcare model and shaping it for the future — a future where innovation thrives, prevention is much stronger and personalised, and Europeans have access to the best treatments.

Full text, original language (EN): Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection, 3 Dec 2025

Good afternoon, ladies and gentlemen, d ear Agustin, thank you for the invitation.

Last time I spoke at a BEUC event, this was via video message on the occasion of your General Assembly in May. This time, I am delighted to be able to be with you in person.

It gives me the opportunity to present to you – and discuss with you – our newly-adopted 2030 Consumer Agenda, which sets our vision for consumer policy for the next five years.

I would like to begin by thanking BEUC and its members for your continuous support and your valuable contributions during the preparation of this Consumer Agenda. Your experience and expertise have guided my team in setting the next policy priorities, shaping what I consider to be a thorough and ambitious consumer strategy. This serves as another testament to the vital role a robust and independent consumer movement plays in promoting consumer rights in the EU, with BEUC being an integral component of this effort.

So, what is the backdrop to our new Agenda?

The title of today's event – consumer policy in changing times – captures it very well.

We are indeed experiencing turbulent times: t he escalating cost of living continues to strain consumer budgets, with its downstream effects intensifying economic pressures and endangering social cohesion. The green transition is moving forward but – as we turn promises into progress - there is still much to be done to balance the demand for consumption with the need for sustainable living. The rise in unfair online commercial practices and the seemingly rapid growth of low-value products sold on non-EU e-commerce platforms are contributing to: challenges for our consumer protection framework and our enforcement capacities, new vulnerabilities for consumers due to pressure-selling , non-compliance , and dangerous products, and unfair competition for compliant businesses – threating the integrity of the Single Market's level playing field. And these challenges are all amplified by the uncertainties of this era of economic competition and geopolitical confrontation . But even in this context of increasing uncertainty, we remain clear and confident in our convictions .

Two weeks ago, the Commission unveiled the 2030 Consumer Agenda , setting the course for our consumer protection initiatives over the next five years. The agenda underscores the EU's commitment to ensure the highest level of consumer protection in the world. By implementing this agenda, the EU aims to support its 450 million consumers who form the backbone of the Single Market —where consumer spending comprises 51% of the EU's GDP. When consumers feel secure and confident, they drive a robust and vibrant economy. And a thriving Single Market is key to bolstering Europe's economic growth and enhancing its global competitiveness.

The Agenda aims to deliver this by laying out our strategic vision for upgrading our consumer protection framework to face these new market realities – focusing on four priorities: removing obstacles for consumers in the Single Market; enhancing digital fairness and online consumer protection; fostering sustainable consumption; and strengthening enforcement of consumer rights. And I would now like to provide you with an insight into each of these.

First , to break down barriers for consumers in the Single Market , the Consumer Agenda puts forward an Action Plan aimed at promoting cross-border access to goods, services, and clean mobility. A true single market is made possible when goods, services, nor mobility have to stop at borders. This is why we want to improve cross-border mobility . Because nothing embodies the concept of a 'Europe without borders' more than the possibility of travelling effortlessly across each and every Member State.

Here, I am glad to raise an initiative which I know BEUC has been very vocal about for quite some time, that being, the difficulties consumers face when trying to book a cross-border train journey. We want to address this next year with a proposal for a Single Digital Booking and Ticketing Regulation . The aim is simple: to make it possible for Europeans to buy one single rail ticket on one single platform and benefit from passenger rights protection for the entirety of their trip. B ut we also want to facilitate consumer access to cross-border financial services .

And we will do this by making it easier to open a bank account or a savings and investment account in another Member State.

Second , the Consumer Agenda commits to enhancing digital fairness and online consumer protection – in particular for young consumers. The digital economy has delivered a host of benefits for consumers: providing them with an unprecedented choice of goods; opening doors to new products and revolutionary services; and saving them time and money along the way. So, as e-commerce spreads, technology evolves, and AI is adopted, we must ensure that our EU consumer law keeps up the pace at providing robust protection , fostering consumer autonomy , and safeguarding freedom of choice .

This is why I will present a Digital Fairness Act next year. This Act is about enhancing consumer protection online, simplifying rules and providing legal certainty for business, and thus safeguarding the integrity of the Single Market's level playing field.

Among several strands, these include tackling: dark patterns that manipulate consumers' choice; addictive design features like infinite scrolling, autoplay, and engagement penalties that capture consumers' time and money; and unfair personalised advertising that exploits consumers' vulnerabilities. The public consultation concluded at the end of October, and we are now analysing the input of stakeholders as we prepare our proposal to be presented next year.

In addition to the Digital Fairness Act, we are also looking at how to reinforce consumer protection against online fraud – from stronger prevention to better support for victims in reclaiming their funds. And we will also work to ensure the fair and transparent use of AI in consumer markets – in line with the AI Act and existing consumer and product safety rules – such as by ensuring consumers receive a clear and transparent notice that they are interacting with AI.

Third , to foster sustainable consumption , the Consumer Agenda outlines the measures taken by the Commission to further tackle greenwashing , early obsolescence , and the lack of trustworthy information about product durability and repairability . It also sets out the rules introduced to make sustainable products more accessible , more affordable , and easier to repair . Our priority will be to effectively implement and enforce these rules.

Looking forward, we will also continue to foster a circular economy , such as “green by design” features, and by promoting the return of goods that are no longer used, second-hand markets, and subscription-based products.

Other priorities on sustainability range from better protections against forever chemicals, or so-called “PFAS”, to better options for switching to more sustainable modes of transport.

And fourth, the last pillar of the Consumer Agenda focuses on strengthening the enforcement of consumer rights. What we are seeing in the surge of low-cost exports directly from third country e-commerce platforms are: unfair or misleading practices – such as fake discounts , unfair personalisation , and dark patterns ; and unsafe or dangerous products that are non-compliant with EU protections or contain banned substances. This creates challenges for consumers, and for business: Consumers are misled , pressure-sold , and even subjected to risks to their health and well-being.

Business are subjected to unfair competition by non-compliant businesses that undermine the integrity of the Single Market's level playfield – in turn, harming compliant EU companies . We have already made progress in addressing these emerging and evolving challenges. An important example of this is the recent agreement to remove the de minimis threshold . However, there is more to be done and here I would like to highlight two key actions.

Following a clear call from business, consumer organisations, policymakers, and national authorities, we will revise the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation next year. All companies who offer their products and their services to EU consumers should be held to the same legal standards, regardless of whether they operate within or outside of the EU. This revision of the CPC Regulation aims to strengthen and simplify the enforcement framework for authorities and compliant traders, and to make the enforcement of EU consumer law more of a deterrence, and therefore, more effective. We are looking at more centralised investigation and greater sanctioning powers at EU level, as well as more support and better coordination among national authorities. And through more consistent application and interpretation of consumer protection rules, we will increase legal certainty , help businesses lower their compliance costs – and deliver simplification and competitiveness.

We will also propose a revision of the Market Surveillance Regulation to explore further measures to make product safety enforcement more efficient and to potentially establish an EU market surveillance authority.

Finally, I would like to highlight how the protection of vulnerable consumers and the contribution of consumer policy to our simplification and better regulation agenda are overarching priorities throughout the 2030 Consumer Agenda. A n example of this is proposing actions to help address the “unavoidable costs” of energy, housing, food and mobility. For instance, our upcoming Citizens Energy Package aims to deliver lower energy bills for households – tackling energy poverty while also fostering consumer participation in the energy market.

Ladies and gentlemen,

The adoption of our 2030 Consumer Agenda was only the beginning of the journey. To put the Agenda into motion and deliver on our commitments, we will need all hands-on deck. I know I can count on your commitment, on your continuous – and persistent – support to make sure that our 2030 Agenda translates into tangible benefits for European consumers. And through our joint efforts, I am confident we can ensure that the safety and wellbeing of European consumers are protected , that consumer confidence and trust remains high , and that fair competition between compliant businesses continues to thrive on a level playing field across the Single Market. And so, I very much look forward to working together to advance our Consumer Agenda in the years to come.

Thank you for your attention, I look forward to our fireside chat.

Full text, original language (EN): Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection, 2 Dec 2025

Despots fuelling hot and hybrid wars. Digital disruption powering unaccountable algorithms. And disillusion leading to apathy and disengagement.

Ladies and Gentlemen,

Europe's democracies are facing generational challenges. Authoritarian regimes view our free , open , and pluralistic societies as threats to their structures of suppression.This is why they try to meddle in elections, instrumentalise grievance, and derail sincere debate through information manipulation .

Digital disruption , driven by powerful private tech companies, has reshaped how we discuss , debate , and disagree . Unaccountable algorithms amplify content that divides , drive not dialogue, but polarisation , and provide a platform for disinformation .

Disillusion brings disengagement, as citizens grapple with overlapping crises – war, climate, migration, cost of living. Citizens question the gaps between what politicians promise and what they deliver. And this disconnect creates fertile ground for information manipulation , disinformation , and for views that reject democracy entirely.

The threats and challenges facing our democracies are severe and interconnected. Facing them requires a sincere and united response.

Democracy is not a given – it is a daily endeavour , a collective responsibility , and a commitment realised and renewed through action. When judges remain steadfast at personal risk, they stand as bulwarks against arbitrary power . And when journalists risk their careers to report the truth, they defend transparency and accountability .

Democracy is the foundation of the European Union – the cornerstone of our peace, our security, and our shared prosperity. It is our European identity. For generations, we have built a continent where people are free to speak, free to choose, and free to disagree.

But today, that freedom is under siege, in a battle for our values. For the right to elect those who represent us. For the right to freely express ourselves. And for a Europe where every citizen feels heard, because every voice matters.

This is why we have adopted the European Democracy Shield now. The Shield will serve as our strategic approach to protect and promote Democracy across the EU. These next years will be pivotal for facing these threats and tackling these challenges – and that is why the Shield announces a series of actions that will be rolled out progressively by 2027.

The European Democracy Shield responds with three main priorities: safeguarding the integrity of the information space , strengthening democratic institutions, free and fair elections , and free and independent media , and boosting societal resilience and citizens' engagement .

Protecting and promoting democracy must take an active approach that includes the whole of society and the whole of government.

This is because when we defend the integrity of our information space, we safeguard open and informed democratic dialogue. When we strengthen democratic safeguards , we uphold the possibilities and opportunities of choice and change . When we empower free and independent media, we reaffirm the freedom to discuss , debate , and dissent . And when we encourage citizens to engage , democracy lives and breathes through representation and inclusion .

The Democracy Shield did not emerge in a vacuum. It builds on the 2020 European Democracy Action Plan and the 2023 Defence of Democracy Package. And it complements an existing robust legal and policy framework that includes: the Digital Services Act and Political Advertising Regulation that are working to protect the integrity of the information space; the European Media Freedom Act and anti-SLAPPs Package already work to support free , plural , and independent media; and the AI Act already establishes transparency obligations , so that users know if what they are seeing is real or fake.

This is why the Democracy Shield serves as our strategic approach for how the Commission is doubling down on Democracy .

For Democracy to flourish , freedom of expression must thrive . For freedom of expression to thrive , the information space must have transparency , accountability and integrity – offering reliable and trustworthy information from which everyone can form their own view .

The is why the first priority of the Shield is the importance of safeguarding the integrity of the information space. To ensure this, we must counter foreign information manipulation and interference (so-called FIMI) and disinformation campaigns , which often rely on fake accounts , artificial amplification of through bots, or even deepfakes. To tackle these evolving threats, the Commission has announced a European Centre for Democratic Resilience. This Centre is about pooling the efforts of participating Member States, EU institutions and bodies, as well as candidate countries. We will combine o ur collective capacity to anticipate, detect and respond to such threats. In doing so, the Centre will provide a framework for coordination and information-sharing , operational cooperation and capacity-building , and strengthening our situational awareness.

And to support cooperation further afield, the Commission has also announced a Stakeholder Platform , where we will be able to receive contributions from independent stakeholders and wider communities.

Complementing these efforts, the Commission will prepare a DSA incidents and crisis protocol , together with the European Board for Digital Services. This Protocol will aim to prevent and to address major incidents and interference in the information space.

The Commission will also work with the signatories of the DSA Code of Conduct on Disinformation . We want to strengthen efforts to demonetise disinformation , and to explore voluntary user verification tools – so that users can actually know if who they are interacting with online is a real person!

An additional front against disinformation will be the setting up of a European Network of Fact-Checkers based on the highest ethical and professional standards, and boosting fact-checking capacity in all EU official languages.

For Democracies to be legitimate, their representatives must be elected without fear or favour. This requires resilience and representative democratic procedures that can withstand attempts at manipulation and subversion .

This is why the next priority of the Shield is about stronger democratic institutions, free and fair elections and free and independent media.

The conduct and organisation of elections remain, of course, the competence and responsibility of the Member States. And the Commission already offers support to the Member States through the European Cooperation Network on Elections (ECNE).

But under the Shield, we will reinforce this support. To address the increasing prominence of AI-produced content, we will prepare guidance on the use of AI in electoral processes . And to respond to the increasingly impactful role of influencers in political campaigning online, we will also support the setting up of a voluntary network of influencers . This voluntary network will provide a forum for raising awareness about relevant EU rules and for promoting ethical and voluntary commitments – while in full respect of their autonomy in terms of the content they publish. By looking at new developments such as cryptocurrencies, the Commission will also support common work with Member States on the transparency and integrity of funding in politics .

For citizens to be represented, they must have candidates to elect , and representatives to speak for them . So, for Democracy to represent , political candidates and elected representatives must be safe . But, as you are well aware, candidates and politicians are subjected more and more to threats and acts of violence – online and offline. It is essential that no candidate is dissuaded from standing, so that they can represent the voices and grievances of anyone and everyone in our societies.

This is why the Commission will also adopt a Recommendation on safety in politics and prepare a guide of best practices in this field. It will place a particular focus on women and other groups at heightened risk of discrimination, as well as zoom in on local politics where the grass roots of democracy must thrive.

Just as Democracies rely on elected representatives to stand up and be counted on behalf of their constituents, democracies also rely on free and independent journalists to stand up and speak truth to power . This is why, under this second priority of the Shield, we will also strengthen the resilience of free and independent media and media pluralism – key within the system of democratic checks and balances .

But in the face of digital disruption and the dominance of online platforms , in some places we are left with news deserts. To counter this, the economic viability of the media sector needs to be reinforced – and we will do this through: reviewing the Audiovisual Media Services Directive – to modernise advertising rules and consider the role of influencers; reviewing the Directive on copyright in the digital single market – to address the development of generative AI; and evaluating the Broadcasting Communication – to assess whether existing EU State aids rules for public service broadcasting remain fit for purpose .

The Commission will also establish a Media Resilience Programme to support independent journalism and media literacy – bridging current EU financial support to the media with the next MFF. And we will intensify our efforts to protect journalists , including by updating our Recommendation on the safety of journalists and by reinforcing measures to combat SLAPPs.

Democracy begins and ends with people.

This is why the third priority of the Shield is about boosting societal resilience and citizens' engagement by supporting citizenship education and skills for democracy. We want our media literacy efforts to reach all ages and all socio-economic categories – down to youth empowerment and the local level. And we want to safeguard evidence-based decision-making – and so will adopt a Recommendation on supporting scientific evidence and its use in public policymaking .

We will work closely with the Member States across initiatives, supporting access to information , stimulating innovation in civic tech in Europe, and showcasing efforts to promote democracy on the ground. As part of this showcase, we will organise a high-level event on democracy and grant a first-ever annual award for democratic innovation .

Ladies and Gentlemen,

This may not be the generation that built Democracy across Europe, but it must be the generation that guards what we have inherited. We must protect our debates and disagreements with the same determination that first built bridges between our nations. We must overcome division with the same drive that turned conflict into cooperation . And we must remember that democracy is not a moment – it is a mission. A mission we are on, together, each and every day.

We no longer need to look to Europe's history, or even to Putin's Russia, to see what we are risking if we do not stand steadfast behind democratic safeguards, the rule of law, and respect for fundamental rights. The European Union was not simply founded on those values , it was founded to protect and promote those values across Europe. This remains our duty, and we are doubling down on democracy to deliver it. While the challenges may be generational, whether against despots, digital disruption, or disillusionment, I am convinced that liberal democracy will not only continue to survive across Europe, but thrive across Europe.

Thank you, I look forward to taking your questions.
