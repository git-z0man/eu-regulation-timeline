24 Jul 2026Draft candidate EUMSS Scheme v1.1 for Public Review
Our summary: ENISA's draft candidate European cybersecurity certification scheme for managed security services (EUMSS), version 1.1 of July 2026, prepared under the Cybersecurity Act at the Commission's request with ENISA's ad hoc working group and published for public review; it is a draft, not adopted and not law. It certifies one service, not the provider: in this first version only incident response, against a common horizontal layer of baseline requirements (an ISMS under ISO/IEC 27001 or an equivalent standard, supplier control, SBOMs, vulnerability management) plus service-specific requirements, at assurance level basic, substantial or high, always by an accredited conformity assessment body and never by self-assessment. Certificates are valid for at most three years with annual surveillance, and substantial and high require evidence that the controls have operated for 6 or 12 months. Several parts are still open, among them the transition period for national schemes (12 or 24 months), recognition agreements with third countries and the terminology annex.

7 May 2026Draft ECCG Agreed Cryptographic Mechanisms, version 3 (public review)
Our summary: Draft of the Agreed Cryptographic Mechanisms of the Sub-group on Cryptography of the European Cybersecurity Certification Group (ECCG), dated April 2026 and published by ENISA for public review until 31 July 2026; it is a draft, not adopted, and not law. It lists the cryptographic mechanisms the sub-group regards as ready to be accepted by all national cybersecurity certification authorities, each marked recommended (at least 125 bits of security) or admissible (at least 100 bits, with a deprecation date, by default at least until 2033), with notes for developers and evaluators. Post-quantum mechanisms (ML-KEM, FrodoKEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, XMSS, LMS) and hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange groups are listed as recommended, while the lattice-based ones should be combined with a classical mechanism; RSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve encryption, signature and key establishment schemes are listed as admissible at most. The document names no legal act.

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, for the Commission

Madam President, honourable Members, Council, cybersecurity is very essential part of European preparedness and defence. Cyber resilience is not only about preventing attacks, but about ensuring that our societies, our economies and our defence capabilities can continue to function during crises.

The risk of disruption to the Union's critical infrastructure by foreign actors is real. Just last week, we saw refrigeration systems in supermarkets and blood banks throughout Europe switched off because of cyber incidents, and the estimated cost was in the tens and hundreds of millions. But it is not only about the financial impact, it's also about the societal impact. Although the attacks did not cause any significant impact on food security and public health, it should be a wake-up call for everybody: Europe cannot afford to have dependencies or strategic weaknesses that expose our critical infrastructure to unacceptable risks. For instance, we cannot allow risks of foreign interference, espionage or disruption in our most critical ICT supply chains.

And this is particularly relevant in our communications infrastructure, such as our 5G networks. They are increasingly relevant for reliable ultrafast connectivity, data and information sharing, drone detection and also real-time operational coordination. And this is why supply chain security is at the core of the proposal for the revision of the Cybersecurity Act, which is now on the table in this House.

We need a strong, pragmatic and proportionate approach to de-risking our essential ICT supply chains, and we need to act now to de-risk our 5G networks. It has been almost seven years since the EU adopted the 5G cybersecurity toolbox. The time for hesitation is over. We need to be much more decisive and ambitious, including when it comes to setting clear timelines for phasing out high risk suppliers. If we fail to act decisively now, we are not only putting our 5G networks at risk, but we will also carry these dependencies into 6G, the next generation of connectivity.

A strong and cyber-resilient Europe, also needs a strong European cybersecurity agency, where the resources match the mandate and the challenges. In the Commission's action plan on cybersecurity and AI, ENISA is playing an important role by strengthening the resilience of our critical and other vital infrastructure against AI-driven cyberattacks, and by testing and assessing the cybersecurity capabilities of frontier AI models.

All this clearly demonstrates the potential of ENISA. This is also a very concrete example of European solidarity and cooperation. Through ENISA, we can ensure that all our 27 Member States benefit from shared expertise, knowledge and capabilities rather than each having to develop them independently. And this is why I urge the European Parliament to support our efforts to equip ENISA with the mandate, resources and capabilities it needs to support our Member States and protect Europe's critical infrastructure, not only against the threats we face today, but also against those that are emerging on the horizon.

Honourable Members, in recent months, we have witnessed a wave of high-profile cyber incidents and other hybrid attacks, from attacks on public administrations and personal data breaches to attacks on health, telecommunications and energy infrastructure, including subsea cables and, as I mentioned earlier, on supermarkets and blood banks.

These incidents underline the importance of the regulatory framework we have put in place at European level. We can already see the difference that the Cyber Resilience Act will make by strengthening the security of digital products placed on the EU market and in many cases, industrial devices such as refrigeration systems are unnecessarily exposed to the internet, or they are configured with weak passwords or run on outdated software. And these are low-hanging fruit, because many of these incidents therefore demonstrate the importance of the basic cybersecurity requirements set out in the NIS2 Directive. Had these requirements been fully transposed and effectively implemented, some of these incidents could potentially have been prevented or their impact significantly reduced. The challenge before us is therefore not only to develop new policies and new instruments, but to ensure that the rules we have already agreed are translated into real security on the ground.

Because ultimately, cybersecurity is not only about legislation about technology, it is about protecting our economies, safeguarding the essential services on which our citizens depend, and ensuring that Europe has the resilience and capacity to act in an increasingly uncertain world. And that is the responsibility we must now deliver on together.

Full text, original language (EN): Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, 19 May 2026

Madam President, honourable Members, thank you for this opportunity, for this very timely debate. Artificial intelligence can be used for good and for bad, and this applies also in cybersecurity. AI can equip cyber-defenders with powerful tools to protect our critical infrastructure. At the same time, it poses grave risks when exploited by malicious actors, and this has now expanded to a new level and pace.

These new frontier AI models possess high capabilities. They operate at a speed and level of automation that outpaces traditional defensive cycles, at the same time also lowering significantly the barrier to entry for sophisticated attacks.

The new concern is that vulnerabilities can now be identified at an unprecedented speed, reducing what would normally take months to hours, outpacing any human expertise in this field. We cannot be certain that this will be the same for fixing these vulnerabilities, and this is the risk we need to be prepared to address.

In recent weeks, we have had the launch of such models for cybersecurity by two of the most advanced AI players from the US, and it is clear that we will see more and more that kind of model entering the market also from other parts of the world.

In the face of these challenges to cybersecurity, we need to do two things. First, we need to make sure that the EU's robust legal framework is applied. And, second, see if there are remaining issues that need to be addressed.

First, under our AI Act, providers of the most advanced AI models must notify the Commission when they know they have such a model. They must also assess and mitigate systemic risks, including those arising from potential cyber‑misuse.

Second, the NIS 2 Directive requires critical infrastructure operators to take state‑of‑the‑art measures to counter all cybersecurity risks, including those stemming from the malicious use of frontier AI.

Third, the Cyber Resilience Act mandates manufacturers of hardware and software to test their products for vulnerabilities before placing them on the market and throughout their lifecycle. It also requires timely fixing of any vulnerabilities discovered. The emergence of frontier AI models makes the swift transportation and implementation of this legal framework a key priority.

The Commission will ensure that these frameworks are implemented as quickly as possible, and we will also make sure that their implementation fully addresses both the risks and the potential of AI. We have also in place a cyber‑emergency mechanism which can support preparedness testing and scanning, and we will see how to deploy the EU Cyber‑Reserve for the purpose of preparedness as a matter of priority. We are working very closely with ENISA, our EU agency for cybersecurity, and national cybersecurity authorities in the Member States on this matter.

We have not waited for these models to appear to anticipate future cybersecurity capabilities. As part of the upcoming technological sovereignty package, we will focus on ensuring the maintenance, security and integrity of our open source digital infrastructures that are coming under increased cybersecurity pressure resulting from the weaponisation of AI. Our proposal for a revised Cybersecurity Act is designed precisely to equip ENISA and the Commission with the resources, powers, and clarity of mandate that this new threat picture is requiring.

ENISA is still a very small agency, compared both with the national cybersecurity agencies, but also with other security‑related Union agencies. We are also now proposing measures to ensure that information flows faster to those who need to act on it, through early alerts, and also collaboration on vulnerabilities.

To ramp up our preparedness in the face of emerging AI threats, I will also present a list of actions in the coming weeks, bringing together the best EU expertise in AI and cybersecurity, and in doing so, we will work with like‑minded partners.

Should more need to be done, we are certainly open to considering this, and this is where the European Parliament can play a very decisive role in the negotiations on the Cybersecurity Act, which we presented last January. Our proposal is a horizontal framework where we can anticipate these issues and where we need to give the EU Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, the powers and the scope of the mandate they need to help our Member States face these challenges.

So we count on the European Parliament's support in this respect. The Commission has been engaging closely with the providers of the recently launched advanced AI models from the very start to better understand and also anticipate their cybersecurity capabilities and how these companies plan to address associated risks.

In the current geopolitical context, where even the smallest vulnerability in our ICT supply chains can be exploited by malicious actors, AI‑powered cyber‑defences are essential to safeguarding our critical infrastructure. An essential first step in this is the rapid adoption of already‑available advanced cyber‑tools by public and private European organisations for their own cyber‑defence.
