# DRAFT REPORT on the proposal for a regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council establishing a framework of measures for strengthening Europe’s cloud and AI ecosystem (Cloud and AI Development Act)

## DRAFT EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT LEGISLATIVE RESOLUTION

on the proposal for a regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council establishing a framework of measures for strengthening Europe’s cloud and AI ecosystem (Cloud and AI Development Act)

(COM(2026)/0502 – C100129/2026 – 2026/0138(COD))

(Ordinary legislative procedure: first reading)

The European Parliament,

– having regard to the Commission proposal to Parliament and the Council (COM(2026)0502),

– having regard to Article 294(2), Article 114 and Article 173(3) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, pursuant to which the Commission submitted the proposal to Parliament (C100129/2026),

– having regard to the opinion of the European Economic and Social Committee of 23 September 2026^(2),

– having regard to Article 294(3) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union,(1)

– having regard to Rule 60 of its Rules of Procedure,

– having regard to the joint deliberations of the Committee on the Internal Market and Consumer Protection and the Committee on Industry, Research and Energy under Rule 59 of the Rules of Procedure,

– having regard to the report of the Committee on the Internal Market and Consumer Protection and the Committee on Industry, Research and Energy (A100000/2026),

1. Adopts its position at first reading hereinafter set out;

2. Instructs its President to forward its position to the Council, the Commission and the national parliaments.

### Amendment 5

*Proposal for a regulation*

**Recital 38 a (new)**

| Text proposed by the Commission | Amendment |
| --- | --- |
|  | ***(38a) The Union’s data centre expansion can only succeed if it is supported by the communities that host it. Data centres use local land, energy and water, and those communities should share fully in the value they create. Projects that deliver tangible and lasting local benefits gain public acceptance, face fewer legal challenges and can therefore be deployed faster and with greater investment certainty. Member States should therefore ensure that every data centre project deployed in an acceleration zone benefits the local communities concerned, in particular through mutual benefit agreements between operators and local authorities, such as the reuse of waste heat for households and public buildings, local employment and training, contributions to local funds or infrastructure, and access to computing capacity for local public bodies, research institutions and SMEs. That expectation should apply equally to all operators, irrespective of their nationality or place of establishment, without constituting disproportionate preconditions for the designation of acceleration zones.*** |

### Amendment 7

*Proposal for a regulation*

**Recital 39 a (new)**

| Text proposed by the Commission | Amendment |
| --- | --- |
|  | ***(39a) Acceleration zones are the result of significant public effort: Member States plan the sites, secure grid capacity, carry out the impact analysis and simplify procedures. The Union should ensure that part of the benefits of that effort accrues to genuine European businesses, whose growth is essential to closing the Union’s capacity gap, keeping investment, jobs and know-how in the Union and strengthening the resilience of its digital infrastructure. European operators often do not have the same financial resources as large competitors from third countries, which can secure land and grid capacity faster and at greater scale. Operators established in the Union and not controlled from third countries should therefore have a limited period of priority access in each zone, giving them the time needed to mobilise investment. In larger zones, a share of available land and grid capacity should also remain reserved for them for a limited period, so that they retain a genuine opportunity to establish themselves in those zones, even if they cannot commit within the initial time period. So as not to slow down the deployment of capacity, that prior access should be limited in time and lapse without timely follow-up. All remaining capacity should stay open to every operator on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms. For the same reasons, European businesses, including SMEs and start-ups, should have prior access to newly available colocation capacity in those zones.*** |

## EXPLANATORY STATEMENT

Europe has many of the assets needed to be a leading force in cloud and artificial intelligence: world-class research and talent, a strong industrial base, and a single market of 450 million people. Yet it has not turned these assets into technological leadership, nor into European companies able to grow and scale. Other parts of the world, in particular the United States and China, have been far more successful in building an environment where digital innovation can thrive and translate into tangible economic benefits. In the field of artificial intelligence, the Union has succeeded in setting a legal framework that provides the necessary safeguards around these technologies. It has been far less successful in developing them. A number of European success stories hint at what European businesses can achieve, but they remain the exception rather than the rule. The Cloud and AI Development Act should change that: it should reduce Europe’s dependence on a handful of foreign providers and give European companies the room to grow.

The co-rapporteurs are aware that the trajectory of AI remains partly uncertain. There are nonetheless strong indications that it will profoundly affect our societies in all their dimensions: our labour markets and social welfare systems, our cybersecurity, our capacity to innovate and the way our democracies function. These are challenges in which Europe cannot afford to be a bystander.

The co-rapporteurs start from a simple principle: cloud and AI technologies can only be developed in a way that reflects European values and standards if Europe does not merely import technologies built elsewhere, but builds them itself. Rules alone do not create capabilities.

Building those capabilities starts with physical infrastructure. Digital sovereignty requires computing capacity located in the Union, with a growing share of it owned and operated by European companies, and therefore data centres. A sound and healthy development of data centres is the first building block of that strategy: one that respects high environmental standards, is accepted by the communities that host them, and creates genuine value for them.

The lack of technological capabilities in Europe has also created vulnerabilities. Public administrations, critical sectors and businesses have become heavily dependent on a small number of foreign providers. Recent events have shown that access to digital services can be restricted or made conditional for geopolitical reasons, and that data held by foreign providers can fall within the reach of foreign laws. Our critical services must not be exposed to such leverage. The providers on which we rely today remain essential to the functioning of the single market. This Regulation should, however, create the conditions for European players to emerge and scale, so that the benefits of the digital transition accrue to Europeans: quality jobs, greater security and control over their own data.

The Cloud and AI Development Act should therefore give the right signal to attract private capital into European businesses, by creating a predictable market for European sovereign solutions. Investors need demand they can count on; public buyers need solutions they can trust.

The co-rapporteurs share the objectives the Commission has set for this Regulation. They consider, however, that on several aspects the original proposal would in practice fall short of those ambitions. Their draft report therefore aims to make those objectives more realistically achievable. In doing so, the co-rapporteurs have sought a balance between ambition and workability: between the urgency of building capacity and the need for public acceptance and sustainability, and between a robust sovereignty framework and openness towards the market. On the sovereignty framework, their central choice is one common European standard, rather than 27 national ones. The following sections set out how that balance has been struck in the two main pillars of the proposal.

Data centre capacity: faster procedures, without lowering standards

The co-rapporteurs support the objective of increasing computing capacity in the Union, and in particular sovereign capacity. Closing Europe’s capacity gap will require considerable investment, and Europe must remain an attractive destination for capital from all over the world. That expansion must, however, strengthen European players rather than deepen existing dependencies. Permitting delays, fragmented procedures and uncertain grid access are among the main obstacles to that investment. The draft report therefore keeps, and in places reinforces, the acceleration logic of the proposal: integrated spatial planning, combined environmental assessments, single information points and the possibility for data centre projects in acceleration zones to benefit from the toolbox for strategic projects under the forthcoming framework on environmental assessments.

Acceleration must, however, go hand in hand with sustainability and remain compatible with the Union’s climate objectives, including climate neutrality by 2050. The co-rapporteurs are conscious of the growing concerns of citizens across Europe about the expansion of data centres and consider them legitimate. Data centres require substantial electricity capacity, while their water use and local impacts vary considerably from one project to another. Their effects on grids, water and land should therefore be assessed project by project. Faster procedures must therefore never mean lower standards: data centres in acceleration zones remain fully subject to Union environmental law, and their expansion must support, not undermine, the decarbonisation of Europe’s energy system. Where material impacts are identified, they should be addressed through effective measures, including energy and water efficiency, low-carbon electricity supply, economically and technically feasible waste-heat reuse, and appropriate siting. Sustainability requirements should rely on the key performance indicators already established under the Energy Efficiency Directive and its common rating scheme for data centres, rather than on divergent national criteria, and the uptake of renewable power purchase agreements should be facilitated.

Speed will also only last if it is accepted. Where local communities feel that infrastructure is imposed on them, without being able to contribute meaningfully or to receive the information they need to form an informed judgement, trust is lost, opposition follows and projects are delayed. The co-rapporteurs have therefore built public acceptance into the very design of acceleration zones:

Participation before designation. Local communities should be informed and able to give their views before an area is designated, using existing procedures rather than adding new ones.

Benefits for host communities. Regions identified as suitable sites should be able to show what the local community stands to gain, such as investment, jobs and services, and may encourage developers to find ways to share benefits with the community.

Transparency. Consistent and publicly available reporting on capacity, energy and water use is essential, both to inform the public debate and to allow the Union to reassess its capacity objectives on the basis of real data, considering applicable legislation on trade secrets.

The co-rapporteurs also want acceleration zones to strengthen European operators, including SMEs, while preserving investment opportunities and effective use of available land and grid capacity. The draft report therefore gives European operators a first opportunity, for a limited time, to apply for capacity in acceleration zones. Capacity that is not used should be released, so that no one can reserve land or grid connections speculatively and projects are not held back.

The same favourable conditions should apply to the expansion and retrofitting of existing data centres, and to colocation and edge facilities, including those operated by SMEs. In this way, acceleration zones do not become a privilege reserved for the largest players, but a lever for a diverse European ecosystem.

Finally, building new data centres is not the only way to increase Europe’s computing power, nor always the fastest. The co-rapporteurs therefore invite the Commission to assess innovative ways of increasing the Union’s computing capacity, such as the pooling of existing compute resources across Member States and research infrastructures.

The cloud sovereignty framework: one common European standard

The Union cloud sovereignty framework is the core of the proposal. Its value lies in giving public buyers, businesses and investors a single, credible reference for what a trustworthy cloud service is. The co-rapporteurs’ guiding principle is that there should be one common European standard: one that does not weaken over time, is not applied differently in 27 capitals, and cannot be obtained in the most lenient Member State.

The draft report therefore strengthens harmonisation. The assurance levels should establish harmonised, risk-proportionate requirements across the Union. Activities presenting equivalent sensitivity and risk should be treated consistently, without unjustified national diverging practices. To that end, the co-rapporteurs have introduced a non-exhaustive list of the categories of activities concerned by each assurance level, which Member States should follow, departing from it only where the actual sensitivity of the activity justifies it, and explaining why. The applicable level should follow the sensitivity of the activity, regardless of which public sector entity holds the data. To prevent “audit shopping”, assessments should be carried out by accredited auditors, on the basis of a common list of evidence and binding guidance. Those audits should also remain affordable, so that European SMEs and start-ups are not priced out of a framework designed to help them grow.

Public procurement is the most powerful tool the Union has to create a lead market for European cloud providers. Experience elsewhere, not least in the United States, shows that the predictability of public contracts can turn promising companies into success stories: with a public entity as an anchor customer, they were able to invest, scale and then conquer private markets. The co-rapporteurs have sought to make it effective without making it unworkable for contracting authorities.

Sovereign resilience cannot stop at the borders of the public sector. Many of Europe’s most essential services are run by private operators: a power plant, a private hospital or an airport is no less critical because it is privately owned. Many countries outside Europe already make sure that their most critical infrastructure, whoever operates it, can withstand foreign interference or disruption. The co-rapporteurs have therefore taken a proportionate approach, limited to a small number of critical sectors, which genuinely strengthens their resilience while keeping costs and bureaucracy to a minimum, building on what these operators already do under existing Union rules.

Finally, the framework must remain open to trusted partners without becoming a loophole. Opening the highest levels to providers from third countries should therefore be narrow and legally certain: the highest assurance level should remain reserved for providers under European control, recognition of trusted partners should be limited to Level 3 and based on clear and verifiable criteria, and no ownership structure or contractual arrangement should allow a provider under foreign control to qualify indirectly.

Conclusion

The co-rapporteurs share the conviction that Europe must reduce its dependence on a handful of foreign providers and regain the capacity to build the technologies on which its economy, its security and its democracy increasingly depend. That requires European companies able to grow, scale and compete, starting in their home market. The draft report reflects that common ground: faster capacity building that respects high environmental standards and that local communities can support, and a sovereignty framework that is demanding where it matters, harmonised across the Union and workable for those who must apply it. The co-rapporteurs look forward to constructive cooperation with the shadow rapporteurs, the Council and the Commission to deliver a Regulation that matches the scale of the challenge.
