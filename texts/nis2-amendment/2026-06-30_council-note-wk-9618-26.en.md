WK 9618/2026 INIT

LIMITE EN A European Cyber Posture Framework Built on Existing National

Experience, Mutual Recognition and Progressive Convergence

The revision of the Cybersecurity Act is a key opportunity to improve the cyber resilience of the Union by making European certification processes more agile, reducing regulatory complexity and building a more robust cybersecurity framework. In this context, the development of a European cyber posture framework may provide significant added value by supporting the implementation of NIS2 requirements across the Union, promoting convergence, and facilitating the sharing and exchange of existing national experience and successful practices among Member States.

At the same time, several Member States have already invested considerable efforts in developing national frameworks, certification schemes, supervisory practices and assurance mechanisms that have demonstrated their effectiveness over time. The key question is therefore how any European cyber posture framework should be designed in a manner that takes into account existing national experience, preserves the logic of NIS2 and strengthens cybersecurity across the Union without creating unnecessary disruption.

1. Building on Existing National Capabilities

The development of a European cyber posture framework may represent a valuable opportunity to support the implementation of NIS2 across the Union, including by providing a useful reference for Member States that are still developing certification schemes, supervisory mechanisms or assurance frameworks.

However, achieving this objective should not require replacing or displacing national frameworks that have already demonstrated their effectiveness. Several Member States have invested significant legislative, administrative and financial resources over many years in developing certification schemes, supervisory practices, assurance mechanisms and ecosystems of auditors, conformity assessment bodies and competent authorities. These frameworks represent a substantial body of practical experience, institutional knowledge and operational capability that should be regarded as an asset for the Union as a whole.

The same principle that is guiding the development of European cybersecurity certification schemes for ICT products, services and processes should apply in this context. European schemes should build upon existing experience, best practices and successful national frameworks wherever these already exist, rather than creating entirely new structures in parallel or replacing mechanisms that have proven effective in practice.

Particular attention should also be paid to the significant efforts already undertaken by Member States in implementing NIS2. Where national certification schemes or supervisory frameworks have already been established to support NIS2 implementation, a subsequent shift towards a European cyber posture framework should avoid creating a second cycle of adaptation, investment and transition within only a few years.

The objective should therefore not be to replace mature national frameworks, but to preserve their full effectiveness and legal effects while using them as building blocks and proven models that can be replicated, adapted or integrated into a coherent European cyber posture framework that supports convergence and facilitates implementation across the Union.

2. Preserving National Responsibilities in Sensitive Areas

The proposed framework should fully respect Member States’ responsibilities in areas closely linked to public functions, critical infrastructures and national security. This is particularly relevant where such activities are organized and supervised at national level or are closely connected to national security, which remains the sole responsibility of each Member State pursuant to Article 4(2) TEU.

This applies in particular to public sector entities, nationally designated critical entities and providers supporting public functions, critical entities, national security or national cybersecurity frameworks related to NIS2 or CER. In such cases, cybersecurity requirements o(cid:332)en form part of broader national security, resilience and supervisory frameworks that cannot be fully dissociated from Member States’ responsibilities.

The European certification framework should therefore ensure that Member States retain the ability to determine the appropriate requirements, assurance levels and supervisory arrangements applicable to such entities and activities in accordance with the principle of minimum harmonization.

Particular attention should also be given to the protection of sensitive information and, where relevant, classified information. The certification of cyber posture may require access to highly sensitive information relating to the internal organization, system architecture, risk management and functioning of the entities concerned. Appropriate safeguards should therefore be established to ensure that Member States retain effective control over strategic information and are able to define appropriate levels of protection according to their own security needs.

3. Ensuring Coherence with the Existing Union Cybersecurity Framework

3.1 Preserving the Risk-Based Supervisory Logic of NIS2

Under the current framework, European certification complements national supervisory mechanisms by providing a means of demonstrating compliance with specific requirements, while preserving the central role of risk-based supervision carried out by competent authorities. Cyber posture certification should preserve this logic and remain an auxiliary and complementary instrument within the NIS2 model, without replacing national supervision.

Risk management and supervision rely on contextualized assessments carried out by competent national authorities, which are familiar with the institutional frameworks, critical sectors, national supervisory arrangements, public-sector responsibilities and national security sensitivities of each Member State. This approach makes it possible to adapt cybersecurity requirements to the specific characteristics of each national environment, while preserving a degree of contextualization that may not always be fully achievable through a uniform European certification scheme for entities.

This consideration is particularly relevant in relation to entities handling classified information, public sector entities, nationally designated critical entities, strategic infrastructures, and their providers insofar as they deliver specific public services or provide support to critical entities, national cybersecurity frameworks or CER-regulated activities.

If cyber posture certification were to become the main mechanism for assessing the organizational security level of entities, it could shi(cid:332) the balance of the NIS2 model from risk-based national supervision towards a model in which certification acquires a central role in compliance assessment. In such a scenario, the issue would not lie solely in the formal coexistence of certification and supervision, but in the risk of altering the underlying logic of the NIS2 model, with implications for system coherence, national supervisory responsibilities and the effectiveness of the risk-based approach.

Accordingly, the determination of what should be assessed, supervised or subject to additional requirements should remain primarily within the remit of competent national authorities, in accordance with the risk-based approach established by NIS2. The framework should therefore preserve the central role of competent national authorities in assessing and supervising cybersecurity requirements and avoid transforming certification into a central mechanism for assessing compliance with NIS2.

3.2 Consistency with the Minimum Harmonization Model

NIS2, DORA and the CER Directive rely on strong supervisory frameworks and, specifically for NIS2, on a minimum harmonization approach. Any cyber posture framework should remain fully consistent with that approach and should not lead, directly or indirectly, to a de facto model of maximum harmonization that limits the ability of Member States to maintain or introduce additional cybersecurity requirements where necessary.

This approach is also reflected in existing Union instruments. In particular, recital 7 of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 expressly recognizes the use of national frameworks as a means of demonstrating compliance with NIS2 cybersecurity risk-management requirements. Any cyber posture framework should therefore be designed in a manner that remains compatible with such national frameworks and avoids undermining their practical role.

This is particularly important where the establishment or mandatory use of a European cyber posture scheme could be justified by reference to an alleged insufficient level of cybersecurity. Such situations should be assessed on the basis of objective, proportionate and verifiable criteria, and should not be inferred solely from the absence of certification under a European scheme, especially where national frameworks or certification schemes already provide comparable levels of assurance.

Otherwise, a structural inconsistency could arise whereby Member States are required to develop strategies, capabilities and national frameworks under NIS2, while at the same time their ability to rely on those frameworks in practice is progressively reduced.

The objective of European harmonization should therefore be to promote progressive convergence between national approaches rather than the displacement of existing frameworks that already contribute effectively to raising cybersecurity levels across the Union.

The framework should therefore be designed in a manner that complements existing supervisory structures rather than replacing them.

3.3 National Differentiation and Extension Profiles

The proposal itself recognizes, through the concept of extension profiles, that Member States may need to reflect more specific national requirements. This implicitly acknowledges that a single European framework may not be capable of addressing all national security and regulatory needs and that a certain degree of differentiation between Member States is inherent to the system.

The framework should therefore ensure that mechanisms for national differentiation remain effective in practice and that Member States retain sufficient flexibility to address their specific requirements.

4. Ensuring a Proportionate and Market-Friendly Model

Even where certification remains voluntary, experience shows that certification schemes may rapidly become de facto market requirements through contractual arrangements and supply-chain expectations. Particular attention should therefore be paid to proportionality and market impacts.

The proposed governance and funding arrangements for certification schemes should remain aligned with the objective of maintaining regulatory neutrality and should avoid creating incentives that could favour unnecessary expansion of certification requirements or encourage over-certification.

Likewise, the framework should avoid creating disproportionate burdens for small and medium-sized enterprises and should seek to preserve a diverse and competitive certification ecosystem.

5. Regulatory Stability and Investment Protection

Following the adoption of NIS2, Member States have devoted significant legislative, administrative and financial resources to its transposition and implementation. In several cases, this has included the development of national supervisory mechanisms, assurance frameworks and certification schemes supporting compliance with NIS2 requirements.

A European cyber posture framework should build upon these existing investments and capabilities. A sudden shi(cid:332) towards a new model that requires substantial redesign of recently established frameworks would risk creating a second cycle of adaptation, investment and transition only a few years a(cid:332)er the implementation of NIS2.

Regulatory stability and predictability are essential to ensure efficient implementation, preserve institutional knowledge and maximize the value of investments already made by Member States.

6. Proposal

In light of the above considerations, it is necessary to review the scope and design of the certification of the cyber posture of entities and its relationship with the minimum harmonization model established by NIS2.

Such a framework should build upon existing national experience and capabilities rather than replace frameworks that have already demonstrated their effectiveness in practice.

Cyber posture certification should not alter the regulatory logic of the NIS2 Directive nor replace national risk-based supervisory mechanisms. Its role should be limited to that of a complementary and voluntary instrument, without becoming a central mechanism for assessing regulatory compliance.

Accordingly, European cybersecurity certification schemes concerning the cyber posture of entities should not apply to public sector entities, entities designated as critical, vital or strategic under Union or national law, or providers insofar as they deliver services, systems, processes or functions directly supporting public functions, critical entities, critical infrastructure, national security, or national cybersecurity and resilience frameworks established under NIS2, CER or national law.

The specific exclusion of the providers referred to above should not prevent those providers from seeking European cyber posture certification for other services offered in the internal market, in particular private or cross-border services, provided that such certification does not interfere with national requirements applicable to the relevant public or critical services.

Cyber posture certification should be primarily addressed to private undertakings operating in the internal market that seek a voluntary EU-level trust signal regarding their general cybersecurity maturity, in particular where they provide cross-border services.

It may also be relevant for other entities operating in non-critical or less regulated sectors, and SMEs or mid-sized companies seeking a market-recognized assurance label, provided that the scheme remains proportionate, affordable and genuinely voluntary, and does not become a de facto mandatory requirement for market access.

It should be expressly guaranteed that any European cyber posture scheme will neither replace nor limit the competences of national authorities regarding supervision, inspections and the adoption of corrective measures.

Furthermore, Member States must retain the ability to maintain or introduce additional national requirements where necessary to ensure higher levels of cybersecurity, in line with the minimum harmonization approach established by the NIS2 Directive.

Likewise, the system should establish a principle of mutual recognition among Member States (“certify once, accepted EU-wide”), based on the substantial equivalence of national schemes, assessed according to clear and predefined criteria at European level.

In addition, the framework should incorporate effective mechanisms for equivalence and recognition between existing and future national and European schemes, ensuring that national frameworks providing substantially comparable levels of assurance may be recognized as producing equivalent effects within the Union, in a manner comparable to article 87 of the CSA, which provides for recognition of third-country certifications to produce effects within the Union.

This approach must guarantee effective coexistence between the European framework and national schemes or frameworks, while promoting convergence through the reuse of existing expertise, supervisory practices and successful national experiences.

The implementation of this model must in all cases respect the principle of proportionality, avoiding unnecessary or disproportionate burdens and facilitating market access, particularly for small and medium-sized enterprises.

Finally, any mechanism intended to address situations of an alleged insufficient level of cybersecurity should fully respect the principle of subsidiarity, be based on objective and verifiable criteria and should not lead to the automatic imposition of European schemes replacing decisions taken by Member States in the exercise of their competences.

7. Conclusion

The objective should not be to replace what already works, but to build upon it. A European cyber posture framework should strengthen cybersecurity across the Union by leveraging existing national experience, preserving the logic of NIS2 and supporting convergence through cooperation, interoperability and mutual recognition, rather than replacement.
