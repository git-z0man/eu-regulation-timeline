About ENISA

The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, is the Union’s agency dedicated to achieving a high common level of cybersecurity across Europe. Established in 2004 and strengthened by the EU Cybersecurity Act, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity contributes to EU cyber policy, enhances the trustworthiness of ICT products, services and processes with cybersecurity certification schemes, cooperates with Member States and EU bodies, and helps Europe prepare for the cyber challenges of tomorrow. Through knowledge sharing, capacity building and awareness raising, the Agency works together with its key stakeholders to strengthen trust in the connected economy, to boost resilience of the Union’s infrastructure, and, ultimately, to keep Europe’s society and citizens digitally secure.

More information about ENISA and its work can be found here: www.enisa.europa.eu.

2.3.2 Selected High-level Findings

There seems to be convergence among MS with most of them achieving a mature cybersecurity posture, which signals progress towards achieving a common high level of cybersecurity. This is further reinforced by the fact that even the average of the five lowest scoring MS is relatively high.

Policy implementation at the national level is progressing across the EU. The majority of MS achieved a mature or advanced posture on the coverage and implementation of objectives in national cybersecurity strategies (66.72 points), highlighting their increased alignment. There are, however, still gaps between strategic intent and actual execution in some areas.

A high maturity posture for the majority of MS was also observed in the implementation of incident reporting provisions (71.06 points) and in vulnerability disclosure policies (79.80 points). However, the latter is one of the indicators where MS are the least aligned, reflecting uneven progress. Despite the high EU average maturity, the increased risk introduced by advances in AI further highlights the importance of progressing the implementation of policy even further. Collaboration among and with some actors is still under development. Supply chain security, especially when cross-border, is a complex matter with implications extending beyond technical aspects. In the EU market, additional challenges may arise due to dependencies on foreign providers of technology, and exposure to strategic risks in specific sectors, underscoring the importance of tech sovereignty.

Several tools that have been designed to address the cybersecurity of supply chains. At the beginning of 2026, the NIS Cooperation Group adopted the EU ICT Supply Chain Security Toolbox ^(111). This provides a common, non-legally binding framework for preparing and conducting EU-level coordinated security risk assessments of critical supply chains.

In addition, supply chain security elements embedded in EU legislation have started or continue to be implemented. In particular, vulnerability notifications are now more systematically reflected in cybersecurity legislation, including NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). This is especially important as vulnerability exploitation remains a key vector for initial access and an increasing industrialisation of the discovery of vulnerabilities is being driven by frontier AI. Advances in EU legislation are accompanied by national efforts as vulnerability disclosure policies are becoming more embedded in national cybersecurity frameworks, although unevenly. Developments in the EU cybersecurity certification can also offer solutions, as reflected by the enlargement in the scope of certification to security services managed by third parties.

Despite some increase in digital skills, gaps in cybersecurity awareness persist and continue to affect a significant share of the population, suggesting that current approaches may not suffice, at least in the short term.

The integration of cybersecurity into national educational curricula at the primary and secondary levels remains limited and uneven. This is weakening resilience among future generations.

The cybersecurity skills gap has increased, with a perceived shift from staff to skills shortage. On the demand side, the gap is driven by the inability to find candidates as well as budget constraints. On the supply side, despite measures promoting cybersecurity in higher levels of education, the increase in graduates has slowed down. While investments are key, diverse approaches such as skills attestation, competitions and bootcamps, seem to be promising ways to address the skills shortage. IV.

Looking ahead IV.

Looking Ahead

The overarching message of this Report is one of cautious confidence. The EU has demonstrated the political will and the capacity to cooperate to build a high common level of cybersecurity. Now it requires an acceleration of efforts. The challenge is to maintain the momentum with swiftness and flexibility to ensure the EU’s readiness as the threat landscape and technology evolve.

Compared to the previous reporting period, the EU has experienced an overall improvement in terms of cybersecurity posture. The October 2024 transposition deadline of the NIS2 Directive played the role of a catalyst in national efforts devoted to structuring and operationalising the EU’s cybersecurity governance. The priority for the period ahead lies in progressing the implementation of the legislative framework, including by addressing its more complex and demanding aspects.

The latter are especially important. Technological developments and shifts in global geopolitics are happening at an accelerated pace and with disruptive consequences: in 2026 alone the impact of frontier AI models on cybersecurity became increasingly apparent, while existing conflicts intensified and new ones took place. As uncertainty unfolds, cybersecurity remains a collective effort and the EU as a whole can provide a more solid front for what the future holds.

ABOUT ENISA

The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, ENISA, is the Union’s agency dedicated to achieving a high common level of cybersecurity across Europe. Established in 2004 and strengthened by the EU Cybersecurity Act, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity contributes to EU cyber policy, enhances the trustworthiness of ICT products, services and processes with cybersecurity certification schemes, cooperates with Member States and EU bodies, and helps Europe prepare for the cyber challenges of tomorrow. Through knowledge sharing, capacity building and awareness raising, the Agency works together with its key stakeholders to strengthen trust in the connected economy, to boost resilience of the Union’s infrastructure, and, ultimately, to keep Europe’s society and citizens digitally secure. More information about ENISA and its work can be found here: www.enisa.europa.eu.
