A.1 How this package supports CRA obligations 20 Scope and context

Manufacturers of products with digital elements need practical ways to receive, assess and communicate information about vulnerabilities. Clear reporting channels, structured disclosure processes and timely security communications are important parts of effective vulnerability management. In this context, tools and practices such as security.txt, Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) and security advisories can help organisations handle vulnerability information in a consistent and transparent manner.

The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) introduces new vulnerability management obligations for manufacturers of products with digital elements. Micro-, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) may need practical guidance and support to put these obligations into practice, particularly where cybersecurity, compliance or legal resources are limited. This package provides practical, ready-to-use templates that organisations can adapt to their needs and level of maturity. It also supports the practical application of the CRA’s vulnerability management requirements and the harmonised standard EN 40000-1-3 on Vulnerability Handling, under development by CEN/CLC/JTC13.

The purpose of this ENISA Technical Advisory is to provide practical support to manufacturers of products with digital elements, particularly SMEs, in implementing vulnerability reporting, coordinated disclosure and security communication practices in line with the CRA’s vulnerability management requirements

In particular, this package:

- • explains how security.txt can help external parties identify where and how to report vulnerabilities;

- • supports the use of CVD practices for receiving, assessing and managing vulnerability reports;

- • provides templates for security advisories to communicate vulnerabilities, mitigation measures and fixes to users; and • helps organisations translate regulatory and standards-based expectations into practical steps.

The scope of this package is limited to the practical use of documentation and processes for vulnerability reporting, coordinated disclosure and external security communication. It focuses on how organisations can publish contact information, handle incoming reports, communicate with reporters, and inform users about relevant vulnerabilities and remediation.

This package does not provide legal advice and does not replace official guidance on the CRA. It also does not cover all aspects of vulnerability management or product security, such as secure development, internal remediation workflows, incident response, or vulnerability handling by third-party suppliers. These topics remain important but are outside the main scope of this package.

Relevance under NIS2: Certain manufacturers are also subject to Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2). As a general rule, medium- and large-sized enterprises listed as types of entities in the sectors set out in Annex I and II are in scope of the NIS2. In particular, Annex II, point (5), covers the sector “manufacturing”. As part of the cybersecurity risk-management obligation, Article 21(2)(e) NIS2 requires entities in scope to address vulnerability handling and disclosure for their own network and information systems. One security.txt file and one monitored reporting channel can serve both the CRA and NIS2. The CVD policy should, however, make clear whether a report concerns the organisation's products with digital elements notified under Article 14 CRA or its network and information systems notified under Article 23 NIS2.

1. security.txt, CVD policy and

Security Advisory

The CRA requires manufacturers of products with digital elements to establish processes for handling vulnerabilities during the product’s support period. This includes putting in place and enforcing a coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD) policy, providing a contact mechanism for reporting vulnerabilities, and publicly disclosing information about fixed vulnerabilities. While the regulation establishes the applicable legal obligation and the upcoming harmonised standard (prEN 40000-1-3, currently under development) is intended to provide further technical requirements and guidance, this package aims to bridge the implementation gap by providing ready-to-use artifacts that support manufacturers, notably SMEs, in addressing and remediating vulnerabilities without delay, while also facilitating the future application of the harmonised standard.

1.1 security.txt

A security.txt provides a standardised way for organisations to publish security contact information, making it easier for security researchers to report vulnerabilities. Defined in RFC 9116, it supports vulnerability reporting by providing a discoverable point of contact and links to relevant vulnerability disclosure policies where applicable. Section 4.1 of this document provides a ready-to-deploy security.txt template. To adapt and deploy the template:

- ▪ Copy the ready to deploy file provided, replace the placeholders with your real contact details and policy URL. Include the PGP key link only if a key has been set up.

- ▪ Use stable, role-based contact addresses and URLs (for example security@yourdomain.example and /security). The single point of contact also has to be stated in the information and instructions to the user (Annex II, point 2 CRA) and should remain valid for the lifetime of the product.

- ▪ Upload it to https://yourdomain.example/.well-known/security.txt and keep in mind that the /.well-known/ path is mandatory, not optional.

- ▪ Set the Expires field no more than 12 months out, and put a reminder in your calendar to renew it.

1.2 CVD policy

A Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) policy explains how an organisation receives, assesses and manages vulnerability reports from security researchers and other external parties. It sets out how to report a vulnerability, what reporters can expect from the organisation, and how information will be communicated throughout the disclosure process. Section 4.2. provides a ready-to-deploy CVD policy template. To adapt and deploy the template:

- ▪ Replace every bracketed placeholder: company name, in-scope products, contact email, and the response-time figures (defaults are provided; tighten or loosen based on your engineering capacity).

- ▪ Confirm the scope list matches your actual product range, including firmware/software versions still supported.

- ▪ Publish the finished policy on your website, and link to it from your security.txt file.

- ▪ Assign one named policy owner and at least one deputy, use a shared, role-based mailbox rather than a personal one, and set a calendar reminder for the annual review.

- ▪ Ensure the policy is implemented in practice through the necessary internal processes and responsibilities. Publication alone is not sufficient.

1.3 Security advisory

A security advisory informs users about a security vulnerability affecting a product, its potential impact, and the steps they can take to reduce the risk. It helps manufacturers communicate relevant security information clearly and promptly, including available fixes, updates or mitigation measures.

Unlike the other two artefacts, the advisory template is not customised once, it is reused every time a vulnerability is fixed and disclosed. There is nothing to fill in ahead of time beyond confirming the format works for your team. Each individual advisory is filled in at the point of disclosure using the fields in Section 4.3:

- • Agree internally who is responsible for publishing an advisory once a fix is released.

- • Decide how affected customers will be informed. Where customers are known, inform them directly, for example via a customer portal, service channels or direct notification. Where they are not known, publish advisories on a dedicated page of your website. In both cases, maintain a public index of all published advisories. The chosen channel should be suitable, taking into account the effort and the risk associated with the product.

- • Use the customised and agreed template (for generic template see Section 4.3) for every resolved vulnerability going forward.

3. Good practices and templates

3.1 security.txt implementation guide

Publishing a security.txt file is a recommended practice that enables security researchers to quickly identify how to report vulnerabilities to an organisation. This simple text file, published on your website and containing the relevant security contact information, provides security researchers from around the world with a standardised way to find the appropriate reporting channel. This helps reduce delays in vulnerability reporting by ensuring that valuable time is not lost trying to find the appropriate communication channel.

Even with its simple implementation, the adoption of security.txt remains limited. Recent measurements by the German Federal Office for Information Security ^(2) (BSI) and the French public data platform ^(3) (datagouv) found valid files on only a small proportion of surveyed websites, highlighting a clear opportunity for wider uptake.

While the draft prEN 40000-1-3 recommends publishing a security.txt file, it does not delve into detail about what belongs in it. security.txt is actually a formal IETF standard in its own right - RFC 9116 ^(4), so the field list already exists; it needs to be filled in and be hosted correctly.

3.1.1 Where it must live

Host the file at exactly this path on your primary domain (and on every domain associated with the in-scope product, e.g. a cloud companion app):

[https://yourdomain.example/.well-known/security.txt]

Note: Do not put it at /security.txt only - RFC 9116 requires the /.well-known/ path. A copy may also be made available at the site root, but the file should at least be made available at /.well-known/security.txt, as defined in RFC 9116.

Products with an embedded web server, such as an HMI or an edge device, should direct users and security researchers to the manufacturer's vulnerability disclosure information. Where appropriate, the device's web interface or product documentation can provide a link to the manufacturer's security.txt file or vulnerability disclosure policy. If a separate security.txt file is published for the device's own web interface, its scope and contact details should be clear and kept up to date.

3.1.2 Hosting requirements

Requirement What it means

This requirement is frequently overlooked because some hosting providers automatically assign an incorrect content type to plain text files. Verification of the hosting configuration is therefore recommended rather than assuming the correct content type has been applied during upload. Once deployed, the configuration can be validated by running curl -I https://yourdomain.example/.well-known/security.txt and confirming that the response includes Content-Type: text/plain; charset=utf-8.

However, because redirects can introduce additional trust and security considerations, it is considered best practice to host the security.txt file and any referenced resources on infrastructure under the organisation's control. Contact and encryption links are also recommended to point directly to trusted resources rather than using third-party Uniform Resource Locator (URL) shortening services or unrelated domains, which may reduce trust and introduce unnecessary security concerns.

3.2.2 Implementation notes

  - • Publish the CVD policy in an accessible format, for example as an accessible web page that can be read and navigated using assistive technologies.

  - • In the "How to report" section, include one or more contact mechanisms that can be used to report vulnerabilities. These may include a dedicated email address, web form, ticketing portal, telephone number, or another suitable reporting channel. Where possible, provide options that are accessible through more than one sensory channel.

  - • Check every incoming report for indications of active exploitation. Where the manufacturer becomes aware of an actively exploited vulnerability in its product, it has to notify the CSIRT designated as coordinator and ENISA via the Single Reporting Platform, starting with an early warning within 24 hours, and inform the impacted users (Article 14 CRA). Define in advance who assesses this and who submits the notification.

  - • Target times in the policy are examples and have to be realistic for the product. For machinery and other OT products, a fix may require revalidation, including of safety functions, and can often only be installed during planned maintenance. Where a fix takes longer than the policy target times, provide a mitigation or supporting measures first (see Commission FAQ on the CRA, question 4.3.1). CVSS describes severity, not the risk in a specific operating environment.

  - • The safe harbour statement can only authorise testing of the manufacturer's own systems and waive the manufacturer's own claims. It cannot authorise testing of systems owned by third parties and cannot exclude liability for activities beyond its scope. The legal situation differs between Member States. Reporters and manufacturers both carry responsibilities in a disclosure process.

    - • Distinguish two cases. Information about fixed vulnerabilities is published once a security update is available, with the option to delay publication in duly justified cases until users have had the possibility to apply the patch (Annex I, Part II, point (4) CRA). Where a vulnerability is actively exploited, impacted users have to be informed in accordance with Article 14(8) CRA, if necessary before a fix is available, for example with supporting measures.

    - • Publish the security advisories in an accessible format, for example as an accessible web page that can be read and navigated using assistive technologies.

    - • In addition to a human-readable advisory, organisations should also publish advisories in a machine-readable format such as CSAF ^(9) and make them available through automated distribution feeds, where feasible. Article 14(8) CRA asks for user information, where appropriate, in a structured, machine-readable format. CSAF is standardised as ISO/IEC 20153:2025, and its VEX profile allows publishing statements that a product is not affected. Organisations that do not want to operate their own CSAF infrastructure can use publication services offered by coordinators or sector CERTs, where available.
