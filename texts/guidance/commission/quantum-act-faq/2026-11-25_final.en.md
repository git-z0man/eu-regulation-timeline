# The Quantum Act explained

The Quantum Act Proposal is a comprehensive enabling framework aiming to position the EU as a global leader in quantum. It supports activities related to research, shared infrastructure, industrialisation and market uptake.

## Purpose and context

### Why is the Commission proposing the Quantum Act now?

Europe is proposing the Quantum Act now because quantum technologies are moving from research into industrial deployment, and Europe needs to turn its scientific strength into commercial and strategic advantage before global competitors pull ahead. The Act is intended to reduce fragmentation, align national and EU efforts, support scale-up, strengthen supply-chain resilience, and accelerate market uptake, while also preparing for the transition to post-quantum cryptography. In short, the moment matters because the quantum race is still open, but leadership will now depend less on excellent science alone and more on coordinated investment, industrial capacity and a real European single-market approach.

### Quantum technology is under development, why is the EU regulating it?

The Quantum Act is an enabling framework: it does not regulate quantum but creates framework conditions to support quantum research, shared infrastructure, industrialisation, access to finance and market uptake. It also addresses the transition to post-quantum cryptography. The aim is to create conditions for companies to develop and grow in Europe as the technologies mature and ensure Europe’s security gets quantum-proof.

### Won’t EU support for quantum computers create threats to cybersecurity?

Sufficiently powerful quantum computers could break existing cryptographic methods used to protect sensitive communication and data. The proposal therefore combines support for quantum innovation with security preparedness. It supports research, coordinated migration, standards and risk-based procurement of post-quantum cryptography. It also foresees the possibility for the Commission to impose stronger requirements to accelerate the transition to post-quantum cryptography, if technological advancements require to do so. Preparation needs to start before such computers become available, because migration takes time and data requiring long-term confidentiality may already be collected for later decryption.

### What is post-quantum cryptography and what is the EU doing to promote it?

Post-quantum cryptography uses mathematical algorithms designed to resist attacks by both classical and quantum computers. It runs on conventional computing equipment and does not require a quantum computer. The Quantum Act will support research, testing, standards and coordinated implementation of the EU transition roadmap. It would also introduce targeted procurement requirements, helping public administrations acquire systems that can use secure algorithms and adapt as cryptography evolves.

### How will the Quantum Act support the European quantum ecosystem to become globally competitive?

- The Quantum Act creates a common EU framework to strengthen quantum research, support company scale-up and market uptake, improve resilience in the quantum value chain, and enhance strategic coordination.
- It aims to **turn Europe’s scientific strength into industrial and commercial deployment by supporting the full quantum lifecycle**, from research to deployment, while building on existing EU programmes.
- It also promotes large-scale initiatives such as quantum grand challenges to address major technological and engineering bottlenecks, encourages milestone-based development, procurement, user access, competence centres, supply-chain resilience and post-quantum cryptography preparedness.

### How will the Quantum Act further support the European scientific excellence in quantum?

The Quantum Act will strengthen European scientific excellence in quantum **in the context of the ‘umbrella framework’ Quantum Europe Framework Initiative**:

- by creating a Union-wide **Quantum Excellence Network** that better connects leading institutes, universities, laboratories and research organisations, helping them work together on major scientific challenges, share access to essential infrastructures and support researcher mobility across borders;
- by launching “**quantum grand challenges**” to tackle key technological and engineering bottlenecks through coordinated, high-impact research efforts;
- by supporting **post-quantum cryptography research, and innovation** in areas that are critical for Europe’s technological leadership and security.

### How does Quantum Act forge synergies between European and national programmes? How does it tackle fragmentation?

The Quantum Act tackles fragmentation by creating structured EU–Member State governance and delivery arrangements, while relying as much as possible on existing Union and national bodies rather than duplicating them. It aligns priorities and programming cycles and supports shared access to costly infrastructures such as testbeds and pilot lines. It also strengthens links between research, industrialisation, finance, procurement and skills, so that national strengths can be connected at European scale and strategic dependencies can be identified and addressed collectively.

### What are the key measures the EU should take to stay at the forefront of building a robust quantum computer?

The Quantum Act will help Europe lead in quantum computing by connecting excellence in research with industrial deployment.

It will support:

- major shared research efforts to overcome key technological bottlenecks;
- **access to European quantum computing infrastructure through a common service platform European Quantum Service Platform for Science and Industry**;
- strategic projects and specialised hubs that help companies, especially start-ups and SMEs, develop, test and scale up quantum technologies;
- strengthening of skills, encouragement of uptake by users and public buyers, and coordination across Member States.

## Funding

### How much money is the EU planning for European quantum research and innovation?

The EU already supports quantum technologies through research, deployment and infrastructure programmes. Future implementation would draw on the relevant EU programmes, alongside national and private investment under the forthcoming Multiannual Financial Framework.

While no fixed budget is allocated under the proposal, the proposal empowers the Commission to establish a new Sovereign Tech Equity Fund (STEF), unlocking large-scale equity investments that are needed for quantum and other critical technologies in Europe (AI, cybersecurity, biotech, chips, etc.).

## Sovereign Tech Equity Fund (STEF)

### What is the Sovereign Tech Equity Fund (STEF)?

The recently adopted Quantum Act enables the Commission to lay the groundwork for the establishment of STEF without delay. STEF will be a new equity investment vehicle aimed at the financial support of technologies which are essential for the Union’s technological sovereignty and economic security.

The Union should be able to participate in the vehicle as a shareholder together with the Member States and other public or private investors.

With a view to increasing the leverage effect of the Union contribution, the vehicle could be established in line with the requirements to become an implementing partner of the ECF InvestEU Instrument. The Fund would make large-scale equity investments in strategic European companies and industrial projects, such as quantum, help complement existing EU financial instruments, and help close one of the most important gaps in Europe's investment landscape.

### When will the STEF be launched?

The recently adopted Quantum Act enables the Commission to lay the groundwork for the establishment of STEF without delay. The objective should be to make STEF operational by the start of the next MFF.

## Applications and practical support

### How will quantum computing interact with AI?

Quantum computers would complement classical supercomputers and AI systems. Researchers are exploring whether quantum methods could help particular computing tasks, while AI can help develop and operate quantum systems. The Quantum Act’s access and infrastructure measures would help researchers and businesses test applications.

### How will the Quantum Act help start-ups and SMEs?

Companies would gain easier routes to technical expertise, infrastructure and information on support they may get (access to skills, computing, financing, customers, etc.). Competence and acceleration centres would provide an initial point of contact while specialist hubs would support technical development. Common standards, testing and procurement opportunities would help companies reach the market.

### What is the European Quantum Service Platform for Science and Industry?

- It would provide a common interface for researchers, businesses and public authorities to find and access European quantum computing resources, including emulation, simulation and physical quantum computers.
- It would also facilitate access to software, technical guidance and related services. Access would follow the existing allocation procedures of the relevant infrastructures; the Platform would help users navigate those resources.

### Will every Member State need a new competence centre?

Each Member State would establish **at least one quantum competence and acceleration centre** as an initial access point for public and private users. Existing national, regional or sectoral organisations could take on this role, and Member States could establish centres jointly with other Member States or partner states. Centres would guide users towards technical and business support, skills, infrastructure, finance, suppliers and procurement opportunities.

## Specific procurement rules

### Giving the sensitivity of quantum technologies, will public buyers have to buy European?

For procurements of quantum technologies, the Act would require security of supply, interoperability and lifecycle support. It would restrict eligible providers to Union-controlled entities and entities entitled or authorised to participate under the international-cooperation provisions, including applicable international commitments. This is not a blanket requirement that every component be manufactured in the EU. Specific requirements for critical components could be introduced under the conditions in the Quantum Act.

### Who would have to procure post-quantum cryptography and when?

The requirement would cover certain digital procurements by public administrations to which [NIS2](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/nis2-directive) applies. Its scope would depend on system criticality, data sensitivity, migration effort and the consequences of compromise. The Commission has proposed that this would apply to procurement procedures initiated six months after the Regulation enters into force. Technical requirements would be specified through implementing acts.

### Does the Quantum Act require every existing IT system to be replaced?

No. The specific procurement rules in the proposal concern **future procurement procedures**, rather than imposing a separate obligation to replace every existing system. Where suitable post-quantum solutions are unavailable, procurement would require a documented migration plan. Existing cybersecurity obligations would continue to apply.

## Governance and cooperation

### How would the Quantum Act be governed and implemented?

**Governance**:

- A **Quantum Strategy Board** composed of Member State representatives, chaired by the Commission, would advise the Commission and support coordination.
- A **European Quantum Alliance** composed of stakeholders, mainly from the quantum industry, would provide stakeholder input.

**Implementation**:

The implementation would be primarily entrusted to the [EuroHPC Joint Undertaking](https://www.eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/index_en), or its successor under MFF 2028-2034, and within its mandate which already implements quantum activities today. Its competence does not affect activities in the area of quantum chips, which are entrusted to the Chips Joint Undertaking, or its successor under MFF 2028-2034.

### Will Europe remain open to international quantum cooperation?

Yes. The proposal supports cooperation with international partners while protecting Europe’s security interests and strategic capabilities. Participation to activities under the Quantum Act would follow the relevant funding programme rules, international commitments and the conditions in the Quantum Act. These include arrangements for partner countries and authorised entities, as well as safeguards for sensitive activities. The objective is to combine international scientific and industrial cooperation with reliable access to critical technologies and stronger European capabilities.

## Software, skills and next steps

### What does the Quantum Act say about open-source software?

- The proposal recognises the role of software and interoperability alongside hardware. ‘Quantum grand challenges’ may support interoperable open-source software, while respecting intellectual property and security requirements.
- The European Quantum Service Platform for Science and Industry would also facilitate access to a common, interoperable software stack and interfaces supporting different quantum computing technologies. This would help users work across technologies without imposing a general requirement that all quantum software be open source.

### How will the Quantum Act support skills?

Competence and acceleration centres would help users identify training and skills opportunities, including upskilling and reskilling for early adopters and SMEs. They would strengthen links between education providers, students, workers and quantum companies. The Excellence Network, specialist hubs and regional cooperation would help connect scientific and industrial expertise across Europe. These measures would complement existing EU skills initiatives like the Quantum Digital Academy rather than create a separate qualification requirement for quantum workers.

### What happens next?

The European Parliament and the Council will examine the Commission proposal under the ordinary legislative procedure. The final rules and implementation timetable will depend on the adopted text. The proposal provides for entry into force 20 days after publication in the Official Journal, with a specific six-month lead-in for the requirement on post-quantum cryptography procurement.
