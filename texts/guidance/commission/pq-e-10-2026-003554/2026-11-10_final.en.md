EN E-003554/2026 Answer given by Executive Vice-President Virkkunen on behalf of the European Commission (10.11.2026)

The EU has a comprehensive legal framework for the reporting of compromises of information systems caused by AI systems. Under Directive (EU) 2022/2555^(1) (NIS2), essential and important entities must notify significant cybersecurity incidents, while Computer Security Incident Response Teams (CSIRTs) support incident response. Under the Cyber Resilience Act^(2) (CRA), manufacturers must notify any actively exploited vulnerability in a product with digital elements. Under the AI Act^(3), providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk must document and report without undue delay relevant information about serious incidents and possible corrective measures to address them.

The EU Agency for Cybersecurity ‘ENISA’ supports common situational awareness of threats, including AI-enabled threats. It also produces reports assessing critical sectors’ cybersecurity maturity, such as the annual NIS360 report^(4). In line with the Action Plan on Cybersecurity and AI^(5), ENISA supports resilience by, inter alia, issuing guidance on protection against AI-powered threats, developing a secure testing platform for AI, and supporting an EU Grand Challenge on AI-assisted vulnerability remediation.

The AI Act anticipated risks such as those materialising today. It requires providers of the most advanced models to assess and mitigate risks throughout the model lifecycle. The systemic risks specified in the AI Act and EU General-Purpose AI Code of Practice include, among others, loss of control and risks from cyber offence.

As part of the enforcement of these rules, the Commission has requested information from various providers about how they work with external evaluators, how they secure their models against theft by humans or by AI agents, and about access for external evaluators.
