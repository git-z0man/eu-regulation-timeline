1. Forewords

Foreword by the President

European companies face an increasingly volatile global economy. In this new age, administrative delays and unnecessary costs can determine whether an investment goes ahead, or a new job is created. If our companies are to compete, they need an environment that allows them to move quickly and invest with confidence. That is why, since the beginning of this mandate, we have put simplification at the centre of our work. Our aim is to make our Union faster and simpler, with rules that are better designed, better implemented, and better enforced.

We are already seeing results. Since the beginning of this mandate, the Commission has proposed 12 omnibus packages. Together with our other simplification initiatives, once fully implemented, these measures will lead to annual savings of more than EUR 22.3 billion. These are not abstract figures. These are resources that can instead be put into research, innovation, and creating new jobs – exactly the investments that Europe needs to grow and compete globally.

Delivering on this agenda is a shared responsibility. Europe cannot afford unnecessary complexity, overlapping obligations or additional national requirements that add uncertainty. That is why we are working with Member States on a new pact against gold-plating, to ensure that our common rules are implemented without adding unnecessary obligations that add costs and fragment the single market.

Designing and implementing effective rules also means working closely with those that they affect. During the period covered by this report, college members engaged with almost 1,000 stakeholders through 49 implementation dialogues. By working closely with Member States, regional and local authorities, social partners, businesses and civil society, we can identify problems earlier and improve how rules are implemented.

Finally, for rules to be effective, they must be properly enforced. When Union law is not respected, the Commission acts. Over the last year, the Commission has launched 874 Infringement cases - the highest number in a decade – while closing 499 after Member States resolved the shortcomings identified. This matters because effective enforcement builds trust. A rules-based Union depends on the confidence that rules are applied equally across Europe.

In the coming years, we will continue to simplify and clean up our regulatory framework, remove unnecessary complexity, and tackle gold-plating. A simpler and faster Europe is one better equipped to compete in a volatile world. And it is only a competitive Europe can that deliver where it matters most - in the everyday lives of Europeans.

Ursula von der Leyen

President of the European Commission Foreword by Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis

Europe’s competitiveness depends not only on the policies we pursue, but on how well we regulate and implement those policies. Our businesses — especially our SMEs — need rules that are clearer, simpler and less scattered across multiple pieces of legislation. They also need more efficient administrative procedures, including faster permitting, if they are to invest, innovate and grow in the EU. That is why simplification is at the heart of this Commission’s competitiveness agenda.

Our work to date, including the 12 omnibus packages and other simplification measures, has the potential to bring about EUR 22.3 billion in annual savings for businesses and public administrations. Through these measures, the Commission has proposed making requirements more proportionate to the size of each company, simplifying procedures, streamlining reporting requirements, making greater use of digital solutions, pursuing more realistic and efficient implementation, and reducing the cumulative impact of EU legislation. Step by step, this is producing tangible results: more clarity, greater legal certainty, lower compliance costs, less paperwork, and more time for doing business. Of course, the responsibility for realising the full potential of the Commission’s proposals also depends on the European Parliament and Council, given their crucial role as co-legislators.

Simplification is about improving the quality of legislation and achieving better results in practice. It therefore goes hand in hand with better regulation and improving the way in which we make new rules. This year, the Commission upgraded its better regulation system so that more proposals — including urgent or targeted initiatives — are accompanied by impact assessments. The main policy options and their main economic, social and environmental impacts are now identified at an earlier stage, providing policy makers and stakeholders with more meaningful analysis.

Reducing regulatory fragmentation is also an important priority. Take public procurement, where dozens of pieces of sectoral legislation contain specific procedures, making the system very cumbersome for public administrations and businesses. That is why our recent proposal for a Public Procurement Act consolidates much of this framework into a single piece of legislation. We must continue this work of consolidation and codification in the years to come.

The success of this agenda depends on listening carefully to all stakeholders. The work continues. The 2027 Commission Work Programme is taking our simplification agenda further. Our ambition is clear: a Europe that is easier to invest in, easier to innovate in and easier to do business in. This is not only fully compatible with, but also necessary for, a Europe that protects and empowers its citizens and businesses.

2. Executive summary

The European Commission continued to place simplification, implementation and enforcement at the core of its work, so that EU rules deliver better for people and businesses, and strengthen Europe’s prosperity and competitiveness. In April 2026, the Communication ‘A Simpler, Clearer and Better Enforced EU Rulebook’ further raised this ambition, with simplicity by design, a stronger better regulation framework, an Action Plan for regulatory deep cleaning, and steps to tackle the issue of gold-plating and achieve faster and more robust enforcement.

Our priority is to keep turning this ambition into results. The simplification momentum must be sustained and the timelines agreed in the ‘One Europe, One Market’ roadmap must be met by all Institutions. This second annual overview report covers the period 1 August 2025 – 31 July 2026 and summarises the main progress and results achieved, in line with the agenda set out by President von der Leyen in her letter to the EU Leaders, in the context of their retreat in Alden Biesen on 12 February 2026, and the ensuing European Council conclusions of 19 March 2026. Complementarily, each Member of the College has prepared an annual progress report on simplification, implementation and enforcement in their area of responsibility, covering the same reporting period.

Delivering this agenda requires a shared responsibility of all institutions and levels of government. The European Parliament and the Council have a key role in translating political ambition into tangible legislative results, while Member States are called to match the simplification carried out at EU level, including by ensuring a timely and correct application of EU law, and by avoiding and addressing gold-plating harmful to the Single Market. Sustained ambition at every stage is essential to deliver a simpler rulebook and a more prosperous and competitive Europe.

Key achievements:

The Commission proposed, during the reporting period, six omnibus packages and 15 other simplification initiatives, generating an estimated EUR 13 billion in yearly administrative costs savings, including EUR 4.3 billion for SMEs. Since the Commission took office in December 2024, the total potential net savings of all 12 omnibus packages and other simplification initiatives has reached more than EUR 22.3 billion every year, representing 59.5% of the target for the end of the mandate.

49 implementation dialogues have been held by Members of the Commission. Implementation support has been strengthened through various tools and programmes to help Member States, businesses and stakeholders achieve effective transposition and implementation of the EU rules and policies. More than 60 reality checks have been conducted to gather operational perspectives.

Significant progress has also been made on enforcing EU law, removing barriers that undermine the fundamental freedoms of the Single Market, upholding the rule of law, and ensuring EU rules work effectively to the benefit of people and businesses in Europe. The Commission launched 874 infringement procedures in the reporting period, which is the highest number in the last 10 years. It also closed 499 infringement procedures after Member States addressed the issues identified. Overall, the Commission is pursuing 1909 cases where Member States have breached EU rules.

3. Delivering results: key measures

A. Simplification and cost savings

Since the start of this Commission’s term of office “making it easier to do business in Europe” has become one of the key priorities to unlock the EU’s competitiveness and ensure its continued prosperity for generations to come.

The Commission is pursing comprehensive action to foster EU competitiveness, as set out in the Competitiveness Compass. This includes simplifying EU rules and cutting red tape, with a target of reducing administrative burden for all companies by at least 25%, and by at least 35% for small and medium enterprises, by the end of the mandate.

Several simplification proposals are already in force, including Omnibus I (on sustainability reporting and due diligence), Omnibus II (on EU investment instruments), Omnibus III (on simplifying the common agricultural policy), parts of Omnibus VII (on digital AI component) and part of Omnibus X (on biocidal products). These acts are expected to deliver a swift reduction of administrative burden for economic operators and national authorities. Other proposals are expected to enter into force soon, following the political agreements reached on Omnibus IV (on small mid-caps, digitalisation and common specifications), Omnibus V (on defence) and Omnibus VI (on chemicals).

The substantial progress achieved so far shows that the EU can deliver fast when there is resolve. But challenges persist, including in terms of speed of adoption and of maintaining the level of ambition during the legislative process. This can sometimes undermine the cost savings proposed by the Commission, delay the positive effect of the changes on the ground, and detract from the political commitment taken by the three institutions under the ‘One Europe, One Market’ roadmap to adopt measures to simplify EU rules by the end of 2026.

During the reporting period, the Commission has proposed six omnibus packages that specifically aim to reduce the administrative burden and simplify the EU legislative framework. These packages – called omnibus proposals due to their cross-cutting and targeted nature – are expected to reduce the administrative burdens by over EUR 5.2 billion in recurrent administrative cost savings for SMEs, larger companies and public administrations.

Omnibus VII – digital package aims to simplify the current rules on data, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence. The entire package is estimated to bring approximately EUR 1.2 billion in recurrent administrative cost savings every year. It intends to improve access to data, supporting competitive and responsible AI applications in Europe, through targeted amendments towards clearer and simpler obligations under the General Data Protection Regulation and a consolidation of the Data Act. Targeted adjustments to the Artificial Intelligence Act aim for a more effective application of the rules, and to provide legal certainty to AI innovators. The package also aims to streamline incident reporting frameworks across the Union, cutting costs for businesses, particularly SMEs. The proposed measures will also deliver tangible benefits for citizens, by addressing cookie banner fatigue – a concrete simplification that could benefit millions of Europeans in their daily lives. While the proposed changes on artificial intellingence (Digital AI component) have already been adopted and entered into force on 27 July 2026 ^(1), the rest of the package (Digital component) is still under negotiation with Parliament and Council.

Omnibus VIII – environmental legislation aims to simplify environmental EU legislation governing industrial emissions, the circular economy, environmental assessments linked to permitting procedures and geospatial data. Measures such as streamlining and accelerating environmental assessments or flexibility in the appointment of authorised representatives for extended producer responsibility will reduce red tape and costs for businesses, accelerate permitting, and help public authorities become more efficient. It is estimated that this package would bring approximately EUR 1 billion in cost savings, of which EUR 890 million in recurrent administrative cost savings every year, while maintaining a high level protection of the environment and human health.

Omnibus IX – automotive is designed to lower administrative costs for European manufacturers by improving coherence and consistency across multiple regulatory requirements in EU law governing the automotive sector. It includes simplifying the Tachograph Regulation to facilitate the uptake of electric vans, and the Euro 7 Regulation to ease rules for small volume manufacturers and special purpose vehicles. Regulatory deep cleaning and stress-testing

The Commission is determined to maintain the simplification momentum. In April 2026 it launched an Action Plan for regulatory deep cleaning of the body of EU law. It aims to create an EU framework that provides legal protection to people and enables EU businesses to grow, by tackling undue costs and potential inconsistencies. The action plan focuses on 12 priority-areas for 2026 and 2027: free movement of goods and services, financial services, customs, taxation, health and food safety, agriculture, transport, energy, climate, environment, digital, housing and permitting.

Several significant measures in the Action Plan have already been taken. For example, with the Public Procurement Act, the Commission has brought together provisions that were previously scattered across multiples pieces of legislation. During the reporting period, the Commission also put forward the energy products omnibus and the taxation omnibus, the latter accompanied by a recast of the Directive on Administrative Cooperation. The recast will help reduce compliance costs for EU businesses by over EUR 1 billion annually, while ensuring that tax administrations remain fully equipped to safeguard tax revenues and combat tax fraud, evasion, and avoidance.

The Action Plan is embedded in and builds on the broader drive for simplification, including the work ongoing to stress test the EU acquis. The Commission is screening the entire body of EU legislation to capture its cumulative impacts, potential inconsistencies and potential measures to simplify it. Each Commissioner is carrying out a stresstest of the legislation within their remit, steered by the Commissioner for Implementation and Simplification.

Examples of stress-testing activities carried out during the reporting period include the follow-up to the evaluation of the National Emissions Reductions Commitments Directive, helping to identify simplification opportunities such as clarifying the format of the national air pollution control programme or improving the Policies and Measures (PaM) web tool and enhancing ecosystem monitoring. In the area of nature protection, with an open public consultation, the stress testing of the Birds and Habitats Directives has been launched. The Digital Fitness Check, currently underway, aims at stress-testing the complementarity, efficiency and effectiveness of the EU digital rules, looking specifically into coherence across specific legal concepts and definitions, the cumulative impact of rules, and their governance systems. The ongoing review of Markets in Crypto Assets framework aims to understand whether the current framework is fit for purpose drawing on experience as well as market and policy developments.

The Commission’s work on simplification and regulatory deep cleaning will also be supported by a new high-level stakeholder group, the Simplification Platform ^(4). The Platform will make suggestions on how EU rules and their implementation can be made simpler and easier to apply. It will involve representatives from business associations, civil society organisations, SMEs, social partners, consultative committees and government experts in this work.

In addition, a comprehensive review of delegated and implementing acts has led to approximately 30% of planned acts being deprioritised. The review helped ensure that secondary legislation remains proportionate and avoids creating unnecessary complexity, so as to best meet the objectives of the primary legal acts. The principle of regulatory discipline should, in the future, be central to the assessment of when new empowerments for implementing rules are needed. Simplicity by design

The aim of the ‘simplicity by design’ principle is to improve the quality, clarity and enforceability of EU law. New legislative proposals must avoid unnecessary complexity and fragmentation from the earliest stages of preparation. Legislation should be drafted so that those affected by it can easily understand the policy goal, identify who needs to act and by when, see how new rights and obligations relate to existing ones, and understand how compliance and enforcement will work in practice. Clearer and more accessible rules lead to more consistent implementation, greater legal certainty and a better functioning Single Market. At the same time, digital-ready policymaking contributes to more coherent rules, their smoother digital implementation, and progress in reducing administrative burdens.

Good proposals need to be built on solid evidence. To this end, in its Communication of April 2026, the Commission strengthened its better regulation framework to ensure that proposals, including those urgent or targeted, are built on high-quality evidence and input from stakeholders, and are scrutinised by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board.

‘Simplicity by design’ requires stronger regulatory discipline, with greater focus on the principles of subsidiarity and proportionality and more restraint in empowerments for delegated and implementing acts. It also favours, where legally feasible and appropriate, more systematic use of exhaustive regulations and complete harmonisation in single market matters, to reduce fragmentation and the risk of gold-plating. It promotes future-proof and adaptive regulation by using monitoring and evaluation clauses, as well as sunset clauses where appropriate, so that outdated or unnecessary rules do not remain in force indefinitely. More focused recitals will set out the reasoning behind the legal act and how it is to be interpreted, instead of merely paraphrasing provisions of the act or referencing unrelated policy initiatives or objectives. Greater recourse to codification and recast techniques, and stronger efforts to ensure coherence between sectoral and general legislation, and between new and existing provisions, will also facilitate implementation. In parallel, it will seek to set more realistic transposition and implementation timelines, with systematic consideration of measures such as grandfathering clauses, grace periods and gradual phase-in where appropriate. The Commission will seek the support of the European Parliament and the Council in these respects.

The Commission has started to put these principles into practice. An internal ‘simplicity by design’ checklist now guides Commission departments in their work, from the start of the policymaking process. A network of legal quality correspondents has been set up, alongside mandatory training programmes and updated legal drafting guidance. Good practices and model legal clauses for SME-friendly provisions are also under preparation.

Listening to stakeholders

Implementation dialogues

All Members of the Commission continued to seek feedback from stakeholders to facilitate the implementation of EU policies and the simplification of EU rules and spending programs. Over the reporting period, 49 implementation dialogues were held ^(5) involving almost 1 000 stakeholders from all Member States.

Many spoke on behalf of extensive networks of members and affiliates ^(6), considerably broadening the reach and representativeness of the discussions. The dialogues reflected diverse views from both the private and public sectors, notably companies, SMEs, start-ups, national authorities, social partners, civil society, and research organisations.

Since the launch of the implementation dialogues, over 1500 stakeholders have been consulted in that way. The main insights provided during the dialogues have fed into the Commission’s simplification initiatives, new proposals and greater understanding of the implementation challenges.

Reality checks

The Commission conducted over 60 ‘reality checks’ during the reporting period. They help identify good practices, unintended consequences, support measures, implementation obstacles, and opportunities to ensure that regulatory objectives are met in an efficient manner, including by identifying potential for burden reduction. The checks are conducted with practitioners who apply EU rules, giving the Commission the opportunity to gather direct operational experience and first-hand feedback on how rules and policies are implemented in a wide range of policy areas. This targeted and more technical tool complements other consultation activities ^(7) carried out by the Commission.

Examples of questions addressed in reality checks include whether and how complexity across the EU’s banking regulatory architecture affects efficiency, consistency and the functioning of the Single Market. Practitioners further provided insights to the Commission into their experience in applying the rules set out in the energy products legislation framework. The results were used to prepare the Omnibus XII on energy products. In agriculture, a reality check held in March 2026 gathered practitioners’ insights on the implementation of the Directive on unfair trading practices in the agricultural and food supply chain. The reality check on State aid private enforcement and the use of State aid rules in national courts gathered insights from judges, lawyers, academics and other stakeholders on the practical challenges and opportunities in enforcing State aid rules at national level. In the EU statistics legislation, reality checks organised in 2025 and 2026 looked at the feasibility of reusing data collected for official statistics and highlighted the need to align statistical concepts with those used in other areas such as business or accounting.

B. Implementation

The Commission stepped up its efforts to track implementation gaps and support Member States in ensuring that EU laws and policies are implemented swiftly, effectively and consistently, by issuing guidance, technical assistance, providing capacity-building support and engaging in structured dialogue. It also supported businesses and people in applying EU rules, helping to reduce unnecessary burdens. We placed particular emphasis on strengthening administrative capacity, on advancing digital tools and AI to facilitate implementation, on addressing gold-plating and on promoting consistent application of rules across the EU, including through the work of European agencies.

Across strategic priorities, the Commission provided implementation support for legislative and non-legislative initiatives, turning EU policies into results on the ground. On competitiveness, the focus was on preventing and removing barriers to the Single Market, by cutting administrative burdens for cross-border services and averting new

On energy, we provided targeted guidance and workshops to support Member States’ phasing out of Russian energy imports. On environment, new guidance – coupled with structured dialogues in several Member States – provided greater clarity and flexibility for applying EU water legislation. It also encouraged greater use of simplified cost options and financing not linked to costs, reducing reliance on detailed cost reporting and helping authorities and beneficiaries focus on delivery.

Follow-up measures to the Implementation Dialogues

The Commission translated stakeholders’ views expressed during the implementation dialogues into concrete action, creating thus a direct feedback loop with the effectiveness of EU law. In particular, the lessons learnt during these dialogues helped the Commission prepare several Omnibus packages, including on digital rules and the General Data Protection Regulation, food and feed safety, cybersecurity and data policy, and energy-efficient products. More broadly, we also drew views on how to simplify and accelerate environmental assessments and permitting.

The usefulness of implementation dialogues extends beyond the simplification of existing rules. They are increasingly helping the Commission shape new policy responses to existing challenges, bringing implementation realities and stakeholder views and experiences into the appraisal of policy challenges and options. This has been the case across a range of areas, including the Chips Act 2.0, the EU anti-poverty strategy, the European strategy for the outermost regions, or the forthcoming review of the European venture and growth capital funds.

Implementation dialogues are also driving practical changes in the way the Commission supports businesses, facilitates investment and addresses implementation obstacles on the ground. For example, in competition policy, following the Implementation Dialogue on Important Projects of Common European Interest (IPCEI), we provided advice to stakeholders to facilitate and simplify IPCEI processes. On 24 November 2025, the high-level meeting of the Joint European Forum of the IPCEI adopted a recommendation on facilitating SME participation in IPCEIs. In enlargement policy, building on the Implementation Dialogue on private sector investments, the Commission launched investment calls for Moldova and Armenia, and organised high-level conferences to mobilise investment and turn the EU’s investment agenda into projects on the ground. These included the EU-Ukraine Business Summit, where the Commission announced a EUR 1.2 billion investment package for infrastructure, energy resilience and SMEs; and the EU-Moldova Investment Conference, where investment plans and projects worth up to EUR 641 million were announced for energy, digital infrastructure, education and sustainable agriculture. In the field of external action, the implementation dialogue on people-to-people initiatives helped refine the Pact for the Mediterranean’s Action Plan. It also allowed the Commission to launch an investment call to map European private sector presence and investment interests in the region, with a view to scaling up investments.

Tackling regulatory gold-plating

In its Communication of April 2026, the Commission announced actions to help prevent, detect and follow up on instances of gold-plating in the implementation of EU law, while safeguarding rights and protections. Gold-plating is where Member States set rules that go beyond what is required or allowed under EU legislation, for example by extending the scope of implementing measures, introducing stricter substantive requirements, or procedures that are more burdensome than necessary to give effect to EU rules. Preventing unnecessary regulatory burdens of this kind is a shared responsibility among all EU institutions and the Member States.

This objective has been underlined at the highest political level. In its conclusions of 19 March 2026, the European Council called on Member States to ‘avoid gold-plating and disproportionate national requirements when transposing and implementing EU rules. Following a proposal by President von der Leyen in her 2026 State of the Union address of a pact against gold-plating, the European Council of 15 October 2026 called upon the Commission to come forward with an initiative. The aim is to sign the pact at a European Council meeting before the end of the year. This reflects a broader commitment to ensuring that EU legislation delivers its intended objectives without creating avoidable administrative or compliance costs for people, businesses and public authorities.

In May 2026, the Commission started work on a toolkit of best practices and practical transposition guidance to help Member States avoid gold-plating. We circulated a questionnaire to national authorities to gather information and data on existing transposition practices. We also made a survey available to stakeholders to collect evidence on the types of gold-plating practices encountered in practice, and their costs and impacts. We are currently analysing the responses received and will use this evidence as a basis for the next stage of development of the toolkit.

The implementation dialogues provided a complementary channel for business and stakeholders to flag where national implementation adds complexity and costs to operating across the Single Market. For instance, stakeholders have pointed to additional national requirements and divergent implementation in areas such as audiovisual media, banking, and health data. In the area of taxation, notably digital reporting, stakeholders called for avoiding additonal national layers of requirements. The dialogues also highlighted concerns that go beyond explicit cases of gold-plating, including differences in national interpretation and application of EU rules. These insights can complement the evidence gathered through the Member States’ questionnaire and stakeholder survey, to better target the areas where further guidance or follow-up may be needed. Implementation dialogues and targeted consultations will give businesses – especially SMEs and those operating across borders – a direct voice in the ‘review and remove’ mechanism proposed by President von der Leyen as part of the pact against gold-plating.

The Commission is also taking action to reduce regulatory fragmentation in key areas. One example is the Market Integration and Supervision package, proposed in December 2025 and agreed by the Council in October 2026. It addresses cross-border and supervisory barriers in financial services, helping the companies gain the scale and efficiency required to compete globally. Greater harmonisation, less gold-plating and streamlined regulatory requirements are key to unlocking the full potential of the single market for financial services.

In addition, within the Single Market Enforcement Taskforce the Commission, together with Member States, has continued work to remove the ‘Terrible Ten’ barriers, including gold-plating, and will report developments.

Overall, the Commission is stepping up its work to ensure that EU rules lead to a genuinely Single Market in practice. Better transposition, stronger detection of unnecessary national burdens and fuller use of existing coordination mechanisms, will reduce fragmentation while safeguarding the integrity of the Single Market. In parallel, the Commission will continue to explore the possible use of AI-driven technologies to identify instances of gold-plating.

Europe's businesses will spend less time on administrative work and compliance due to the European Business Wallets proposed by the Commission in November 2025. C. Enforcement

In its April Communication, the Commission took significant steps towards better and faster enforcement of EU rules across all policy areas, together with a specific initiative to boost strategic enforcement of the single market rulebook. Many of the actions outlined in the Communication are currently being implemented, so this report highlights a few specific measures taken in the first months after their announcement. The Annual Single Market Enforcement Agenda ^(13) is further proof of the Commission’s determinaton to enforcement in this area.

An improved Europa webpage on infringement cases, pre-infringement dialogues and transposition of directives now provides up-to-date statistical information on the Commission’s enforcement activities and Member States’ compliance with EU law, including trends per policy area, topic and Member State.

More frequent pre-infringement dialogues

The Commission uses a pre-infringement dialogue, formerly known as ‘EU Pilot’, when it is likely to lead to swifter compliance than a formal infringement procedure. These dialogues also help the Commission to gather information needed to determine whether there has been a breach of EU law. Over the reference period, the Commission initiated 332 dialogues, an increase on previous years ^(14). Overall, the dialogues led to positive outcomes, with a resolution rate of 74% ^(15). These include files in very different policy areas, ranging from financial services and the savings and investments union, to transport, taxation, environment and energy policy.

The Commission aims to help Member States achieve compliance from the outset. But where this approach fails, fast and robust enforcement action is needed so that EU law delivers its intended benefits to people and businesses.

4. Way forward

The Commission has put forward an extensive set of simplification measures across key policy areas. The priority now is to ensure that these proposals translate into tangible results on the ground for people, businesses and public administrations across the EU. The work to simplify EU rules will only be meaningful if it is felt in practice: by reducing administrative burden, clearer rules, faster procedures and more effective implementation.

This requires a continuous shared interinstitutional effort. Progress will depend not only on the ambition of the Commission’s proposals, but also on the speed and drive of the European Parliament and Council when examining and adopting them. Maintaining the simplification potential throughout the legislative process will be essential to ensure that intended benefits are not delayed, diluted or lost along the way. A stronger collective focus on implementation impacts and user experience and clarity should guide the next stage of interinstitutional work.

At the same time, it is important to maintain the momentum for simplification. The Commission will sustain its drive to improve regulatory efficiency, as set out in the 2027 Commission Work Programme and with an immediate focus on the priority areas identified in the Action Plan on regulatory deep cleaning. In addition, the Commission is embedding the ‘simplicity by design’ principles throughout the policy cycle, from design to transposition, application, monitoring and enforcement. ‘Simplicity by design’ does not end with adoption by the Commission but needs to be further preserved by Parliament and Council, and in the implementation. The Union’s rules must be designed with greater attention to their cumulative impact and the realities faced by those applying them on the ground.

Listening attentively to all stakeholders remains central to this approach. The Commission will continue to engage through implementation dialogues, reality checks and other consultation tools to identify obstacles early, understand practical experience and take corrective action where needed – reaching out to a wide set of stakeholders across sectors, interests and geographies across Member States. This constant structured feedback loop is essential for ensuring that EU legislation and policies remain fit for purpose and responsive to evolving needs. It will also help build a stronger evidence base for future policy responses to simplify, implement and enforce EU rules.
