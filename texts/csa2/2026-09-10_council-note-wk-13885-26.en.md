Point 1 – Scope and Methodology General Remark: Due to the wording in Art. 98 of the COM-proposal for a CSA 2, which refers to Annex I and II of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, it is assumed that the CSA 2 Trusted ICT supply chain framework applies to operators of trading venues and CCPs in addition to obligations under DORA. If that is the case, it is propose to add a corresponding clarification either directly in the legal text (for example in Art 98 para 2 CRA 2) or in a recital. If the CSA 2 Trusted ICT supply chain framework shall not apply to entities, which have to apply DORA, the wording in Art 98 para 1 CSA 2 would have to be amended accordingly. Such a clarification is essential to ensure legal clarity for market participants.

Question 1 Do you believe only applying the scope of the framework (Article 98)to address non-technical risks to just the entities listed in Annexes 1 & 2of the NIS2 Directive is sufficient? Does it adequately capture the range of entities that could supply key ICT assets, or components of key ICT assets, incritical ICT Supply Chains to Important and Essential Entities? First and foremost, from an AT perspective the scope of application of Art 98 still remains unclear. It would thus be desirable to provide further clarification and a more precise definition/clearer wording of whom it applies to (in both a positive and a negative sense). Limiting the scope of application exclusively to the entities listed in Annexes I and II of the NIS-2 Directive does however not appear to be sufficient, as critical ICT supply chains also include manufacturers, suppliers, software developers, integrators and other service providers who do – for whichever reason – not themselves fall within the scope of the NIS-2 Directive, but which supply important ICT assets or their components to important or essential entities. Particular consideration should be given to the significance of the ICT assets supplied, their importance to the operational capability of the entity concerned, and existing dependencies and influences. Whilst the NIS-2 Directive impose obligations on important and essential entities, it does not necessarily apply to their suppliers, their technology providers or providers of new technologies. The scope of application should therefore not be linked exclusively, in a formal sense, to Annexes I and II of the NIS-2 Directive. A risk-based approach should be adopted to ensure that, where necessary, the scope can also cover those entities or suppliers that provide essential ICT assets or components thereof for critical ICT supply chains and may thereby pose a significant supply chain risk. Consequently, supply chains – particularly those involving security providers such as quantum cryptography and AI – should be held accountable.

Concurrently, the scope of the current Art 98 might potentially be construed very broadly. It may apply, for example, to SMEs, small public contracting authorities such as local authorities, etc., which operate in NIS-2 sectors. In view of the anticipated obligations and financial implications of Title IV, the introduction of a ‘de minimis’ threshold for cases of hardship in Article 98 – at least for public bodies (such as local authorities, etc.) – would be strongly supported. Irrespective of this, however, it should be noted that such bodies also provide public services in high-risk sectors and should therefore take appropriate and proportionate measures to ensure cybersecurity.

Question 2 Do you believe that the criteria for the Security Risk Assessments set out in Article 99 are sufficient? Should they be further developed or would you like to see a common risk methodology for non-technical risks applied to the security risk assessments? Both Art 99 and the criteria stipulated therein as well as the following Articles on the Trusted ICT supply chain framework, including the powers granted to the COM therein, are rather broadly defined and should be specified in more detail e.g. by adding definitions, examples and limitations. Thus, the criteria are not yet considered to be sufficient. There is a need for clarification on what the specific criteria for security risk assessments actually entail and how they relate to the existing assessment framework under the ICT Supply Chain Toolbox. The MS should have a legitimate role in establishing the exact criteria, a clarification of which could also be foreseen in the legal text. Therefore, while Art 99 provides a solid starting point, it is still considered to be too vague to allow for a uniform assessment of non-technical risks. A common methodology (at EU level), uniform criteria or a common assessment framework and risk categories, building on Art 22 of NIS-2 and the EU ICT Supply Chain Security Toolbox, would be useful in this context. Furthermore, the proposed maximum duration of six months for risk assessments in Art 99 para 2 does not seem feasible. The possibility of shortening the deadline even less so. The prior experiences in the NIS CG have shown, that six months are not sufficient to conduct thorough and comprehensive security risk assessments.

Point 3 – High Risk Suppliers Question 5 Under Article 22 of the NIS 2 Directive and Article 99 of the proposed Title IV, the NIS Cooperation Group is empowered to conduct the security risk assessment. What other EU entities/bodies, if any, should be involved in the risk assessment process, for designating high risk suppliers, to capture the full spectrum of risks in the ICT supply chain mechanism? A role of ENISA beyond its role as secretariat of the NIS Cooperation Group, e.g. also in the Art 103 para 4 impact assessment, could be discussed in line with e.g. Art 5 para 5 and ENISAs extended mandate under CSA 2, making use of its cybersecurity expertise. It could be considered to expressly give ENISA such a mandate in Title II of the CSA 2.

Article 99 focuses exclusively on risk analysis; the choice of mitigation measures lies largely with the Commission – here, too, it should be ensured that the Member States are closely involved, at the very least, in the selection of mitigation measures. For military providers and operational environments, the risk assessment could involve the EUMS (European Union Military Staff, EUMS), and in particular the EUCDCC (EU Cyber Defence Coordination Centre).

Depending on the specific ICT supply chain or sector being assessed, organisations with sector-specific expertise (e.g. sectoral regulators, industry stakeholders, etc.) should be involved in carrying out security risk assessments. Other relevant EU bodies – in particular the EEAS/EU and INTCEN, Europol/EC3 and CERT-EU – as well as sector-specific EU bodies where necessary, could be involved. The role of CERT-EU and its partner nations could be further strengthened. This would ensure that the full spectrum of technical and non-technical risks is adequately taken into account when assessing ICT supply chains and high-risk suppliers.

Question 6 In the current proposal of Title IV, as part of the adoption of the implementing act, the comitology process is used to prohibit use of high-risk suppliers after the security risk assessment carried out as per Article 99 and the economic and societal impacts are assessed by the Commission as per Article 103 (4). Does this process provide sufficient clarity, information and methodology for Member State experts to make a complete judgement during the comitology process? As already mentioned above, in AT promotes that the Council, acting by a qualified majority, should adopt any restrictive or prohibitive measure concerning key ICT assets in critical ICT supply chains. With regard to measures against high-risk suppliers, it is noted that, whilst Art 103 empowers the COM to adopt restrictions and mitigation measures by means of implementing acts, taking into account risks as well as economic and social consequences, the accompanying decision-making procedure (comitology) requires substantial improvement beyond the formal involvement of the MS. It is emphasised that an appropriate decision by national expert bodies can only be guaranteed if the basis for assessment, specific risk scenarios, interdependencies and the methodology applied are provided in a timely and fully transparent manner; the mere authorisation to lay down technical requirements in Art 103 (3) is not sufficient for this purpose. Consequently, it would be advisable to retain the comitology procedure in principle, but to supplement it with binding requirements regarding the scope of information, a uniform assessment methodology and transparency, in order to ensure that MS receive the information necessary for a comprehensive assessment in an appropriate form.

Additional Remarks AT welcomes the goal of strengthening the European sovereignty and independence in cybersecurity matters. Yet, the bureaucratic effort and costs caused by additional provisions in this field (e.g. due to market participants analysing, monitoring, and potentially replacing their ICT components) should be kept as low as possible.

Additionally, it shall be mentioned that, there is a risk that, within the proposed timeframes, it will not be possible to gather sufficient data to provide a comprehensive assessment of, in particular, the economic and other implications of any compensatory measures. Should, for example, the segmentation of networks be proposed, this could have far-reaching implications for the IT architecture of public institutions. It would likely often be impossible to implement this measure effectively within a short timeframe. In order to obtain information on an appropriate transition period, a comprehensive consultation with the relevant bodies would be required, which, realistically, could not be carried out within a short timeframe in the case of urgent measures.

BELGIUM

Q1 Belgium aligns with the view that the NIS2 scope provides a suitable starting point for the ICT Supply Chain Framework. However, limiting the framework to entities listed in Annexes I and II may not fully capture all suppliers of key ICT assets or components that can affect critical ICT supply chains. Belgium therefore supports a more flexible, risk-based approach that moves beyond a purely entity-based scope and assesses the actual risk associated with specific ICT assets, services, components and supply-chain dependencies. CYPRUS

General considerations Cyprus supports the objective of strengthening the security and resilience of ICT supply chains at Union level. ICT dependencies today are cross-border and deeply interconnected, and no Member State can address them adequately on its own. A common European framework therefore brings clear added value, particularly for Member States with small domestic markets and limited capacity to carry out complex supply-chain risk assessments.

How the mechanism is implemented matters as much as how it is designed. Member States differ considerably in size, market structure, administrative capacity and dependency profile, and a measure which a large Member State can absorb may weigh far more heavily on a smaller one, where the choice of suppliers and alternative technologies is narrow and replacing an established supplier is both costly and slow. Cyprus therefore attaches particular importance to a genuinely risk-based approach, consistent with the wider Union cybersecurity acquis and in particular with Directive (EU) 2022/2555, and to avoiding duplication with obligations arising under other instruments.

1. Scope and Methodology 1.1 Question 1 – Scope of the framework (Article 98) The scope proposed in Article 98 provides a reasonable basis for addressing non-technical risks, and Cyprus would not wish to see it narrowed to the entities which formally meet the size thresholds of Directive (EU) 2022/2555. Covering entities of the type listed in Annexes I and II irrespective of their size is the right approach, since a small entity may equally supply key ICT assets or components to essential and important entities. Whether the current wording captures every entity which may supply key ICT assets, or components of such assets, in critical supply chains is a different question. Dependencies extend well beyond the entities which are themselves subject to Directive (EU) 2022/2555, and the text should make clear that a supplier can be assessed whether or not it falls within Annexes I and II. The same applies to the line between the entities protected by the mechanism and the suppliers, assets and components which may be assessed, which would benefit from clearer definitions and consistent terminology so that Member States and operators know what to expect. A further point concerns the relationship with existing rules. Title IV should distinguish more clearly the non-technical risks it covers from the technical risks already addressed in Article 21 of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 and Article 13 of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, which would improve legal certainty and avoid overlaps in implementation. It would also help to clarify how the identification of key ICT assets under Article 102 relates to the mitigation measures set under Article 103. Cyprus would therefore keep the scope broad, provided the mechanism itself is activated in a proportionate, targeted manner and without placing excessive burden on national authorities.

1.2 Question 2 –Criteria and common risk methodology (Article 99) The criteria in Article 99 are not yet sufficient. Cyprus strongly supports developing a common methodology for the assessment of non-technical risks, agreed with the involvement of Member States, as this is the surest way of treating Member States, suppliers and third countries alike and of preventing divergent national assessments of comparable situations. The methodology should also make clear how individual risk factors are assessed and weighted. In substance, it should set common criteria for assessing and weighting risks, and should require assessments to rest on reliable, verifiable and up-to-date information, with appropriate handling of classified or sensitive material. Both the likelihood of a risk and the seriousness of its impact need to be taken into account, and different types of risk and dependency should be treated differently. The existence of a risk and the question whether restrictive measures are necessary and proportionate should be kept apart, with due regard to the technical, organisational, legal and contractual safeguards which may already be in place. The availability of alternative suppliers, technologies and solutions deserves close attention, including the time and resources realistically required to move to them, as does the very different exposure of Member States, since a small domestic market leaves fewer alternatives and therefore higher relative costs. The procedural side of Article 99 also needs work. The provision allows the Commission, or a group of at least three Member States, to request a coordinated risk assessment, but says nothing about the conditions under which the NIS Cooperation Group is to accept or may decline such a request, or about what follows in either case. Clearer rules here would add transparency and keep Member States actively involved. The six-month deadline in Article 99(2) may likewise prove unrealistic in certain sectors, given the complexity and breadth of the assessments concerned, and experience from earlier exercises points in the same direction. Cyprus would prefer to see duly justified extensions made possible. The methodology should of course remain detailed enough to ensure consistency, while leaving room for the specific features of different supply chains, and it should not place unnecessary administrative burdens on Member States with limited resources. Making full use of information and expertise which already exists, together with common templates or practical guidance, would considerably ease the contribution of Member States.

3. High Risk Suppliers 3.1 Question 5 – Bodies to be involved in the risk assessment The range of expertise drawn on should be wider than the current text suggests. The NIS Cooperation Group should remain central to the coordinated risk assessment process, given its expertise and the role already assigned to it under Article 22 of Directive (EU) 2022/2555. What the Regulation should do is set out clearly what the Commission, the Member States, ENISA and the other Union bodies each contribute, so as to avoid duplication and unnecessary administrative burden. ENISA is best placed to provide technical expertise and methodological support, while the relevant Commission services can contribute on economic, industrial, market and trade matters and the European External Action Service on geopolitical and threat-actor information. Where the assessment concerns electronic communications networks covered by Chapter II, BEREC and the national regulatory authorities would have much to add, and other Union bodies could be associated within their existing mandates where their expertise is relevant. Member State involvement should be meaningful but proportionate. There is little sense in each Member State repeating technical or economic analysis which can be carried out effectively at Union level, and this matters especially for smaller administrations with limited specialised resources. Every Member State should nevertheless keep a clear opportunity to provide national information on dependencies, existing deployments, substitution capacity and likely national impacts. Cyprus recognises the need for the urgent assessments foreseen in Article 99(3) and for a mechanism allowing the Commission to react quickly to serious threats. The sequence between consulting Member States and carrying out the risk assessment should nevertheless be clarified, and safeguards should keep this route exceptional so that it does not displace the coordinated approach of Article 99(1). An expedited procedure would be acceptable to Cyprus as long as the common methodology continues to apply, Member States are informed and consulted as early as possible, the assessment is subsequently reviewed through the normal process and any provisional measures are proportionate, time-limited and open to review.

3.2 Question 6 – Comitology process for high-risk suppliers As it stands, the process does not give Member State experts the clarity, information and methodology they need to reach a complete judgement. Experts should have access to the underlying risk assessment, to the methodology applied and to the assessment carried out under Article 103(4), subject to appropriate arrangements for classified and sensitive information. They should also be able to see how the establishment, ownership and control assessment under Article 104 was carried out. Where the Commission concludes that a supplier is established in or controlled from a designated third country simply because the supplier did not reply within the deadline, such presumptions call for caution and for appropriate safeguards. The supplier’s right to be heard on the preliminary findings, and to ask for a re-assessment when circumstances change, should be preserved. [blacked out in the released PDF]

Concluding position Cyprus supports an effective and credible Union framework for addressing ICT supply-chain risks. What matters is that the mechanism recognises how different the circumstances of Member States are and keeps measures proportionate to the risks identified and to their practical consequences. The framework should be strong at Union level, consistent in application and proportionate in implementation, and it should allow smaller Member States both to take an effective part in decision-making and to implement in practice what is adopted. CZECHIA

1. Do you believe only applying the scope of the framework (Article 98) to address non-technical risks to just the entities listed in Annexes 1 & 2 of the NIS2 Directive is sufficient? Does it adequately capture the range of entities that could supply key ICT assets, or components of key ICT assets, in critical ICT Supply Chains to Important and Essential Entities?

- • Yes, the scope of the framework should be aligned with scope of NIS2, i.e. entities formally falling under the NIS2 regime, namely essential and important entities as defined in Article 3 and within the scope of Article 2 of NIS2. The current reference to sectors or “entities of the type referred to in Annexes I and II of NIS2” extends the mechanism beyond entities actually covered by NIS2, which may cause difficulties in supervision and enforcement of the applicable rules, and in the end, legal uncertainty. Entities not covered by NIS2 do not have any obligation regarding cyber security management, they do not fall within the supervision powers of competent authorities and are not registered in any database. It is not desirable for these entities to have advanced obligations (CSA2), but not the basic ones (NIS2). Alignment with the scope of NIS2 will also ensure sufficient knowledge of important and essential entities and their systems by their national authorities supervising the process and therefore suitable application of mitigation measures by these entities.

- • Risks associated with products should continue to be addressed through dedicated product-security frameworks, such as Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). At the same time, coherence between supply-chain risk management and product-security requirements should be ensured in order to avoid regulatory fragmentation and duplication.

- • Moreover, Union entities covered by Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2023/2841 should also be explicitly included, taking into account relevant governance structures and processes, in particular the role of IICB.

The regulation should also clearly set out all designation prerequisites and information sources to ensure clarity and transparency of the process.

- • The processes under Article 99 CSA2 and Article 22 NIS2 should be aligned to ensure that there is one unified approach to supply chain risk assessments, without creating unnecessary duplication and inconsistencies. A detailed common methodology to the security risks assessments should be developed within the existing NIS2 framework, with NIS2 Cooperation Group being the main forum for this work.

- • The methodology should enable the NIS Cooperation Group to transparently prioritise and schedule assessments through a risk assessment roadmap. Such a roadmap should take into account the criticality of the subject matter, but also complexity of individual assessments, realistic timelines and available resources. If the NIS Cooperation Group is to assume this central role, its mandate, resources and working arrangements should be adjusted accordingly.

Question 5 - Bodies involved in the risk assessment

Spain considers that the decisive question is not which additional bodies should join the risk assessment process, but under what procedural regime the assessment is conducted. Title IV changes the nature of the exercise: under Article 22 of NIS2, coordinated risk assessments inform voluntary cooperation, but under this Title, the assessment becomes the decisive premise of legally binding restrictive measures. The intensity of the procedure must match the weight of the decisions it prepares. No assessment capable of serving as the decisive basis for a restrictive measure under this Title should be endorsed through a silence procedure: working methods appropriate for voluntary cooperation (such as endorsement by non-objection, analytical framing developed outside the Member States with the group in a reviewing role, or short feedback windows) would materially weaken the procedural and evidentiary basis of any resulting act. The Regulation itself should therefore establish the essential procedural elements of Title IV assessments, including, among other elements, adoption by an explicit positive decision of Member State representatives, under a voting rule laid down in the Regulation itself in which non-objection shall not constitute validation. It should further provide for involvement of Member States in defining scenarios, criteria and analytical framing from the outset, timelines compatible with national interministerial coordination and the guarantee that a Member State's diverging position shall be recorded and shall not be treated as acceptance of the assessment, nor prejudge that Member State's position on any subsequent measure. On information handling, Spain proposes a layered regime in place of undifferentiated confidentiality or publication:

- • the complete file accessible to Member States through secure channels;

- • specific arrangements for classified and sensitive material;

- • a communicable version for the supplier concerned, sufficient to exercise its rights;

- • and public reasoning of the final act. Until a formal act is adopted, no assessment content identifying suppliers or countries should be published, since such publication produces market effects without procedure or remedy. Where the urgent pathway of Article 99(3) is used, the Commission may initiate and prepare the assessment without delay, applying the common methodology. The NIS Cooperation Group shall, within an abbreviated timeline, either endorse the assessment by an explicit positive decision, thereby making its findings its own, or complete and adopt a coordinated assessment itself. No designation or irreversible measure may rest on an assessment that has not been so endorsed or adopted, and, where the Regulation provides for provisional measures pending that endorsement or adoption, any such measure must be temporary, reversible and subject to immediate Member State control. Urgency may justify shorter timelines, but it must not lead to lower guarantees. Within this regime, other Union entities and bodies should contribute as providers of input within their existing mandates (i.e., ENISA through technical and secretariat support, the Commission services through the trade, industrial and market data they hold, the EEAS where geopolitical context is relevant) while ownership of the assessment remains with the Member States collectively. Question 1 – Scope

Do you believe only applying the scope of the framework (Article 98) to address non-technical risks to just the entities listed in Annexes I & II of the NIS2 Directive is sufficient? Does it adequately capture the range of entities that could supply key ICT assets, or components of key ICT assets, in critical ICT Supply Chains to Important and Essential Entities?

Slovenia supports a functional and risk-based scope covering suppliers regardless of their size where their products or services are critical to the security or continuity of essential or important entities. Suppliers and subcontractors, including SMEs that are not themselves subject to NIS 2, but may provide key ICT assets, critical components, software updates or privileged access to systems of essential and important entities that within Annexes I and II of the NIS 2, should fall within the scope.

At the same time, clearer definitions of “critical ICT supply chain”, “key ICT asset” and “critical component” are needed to ensure legal certainty and proportionality.

Question 2 – Risk-assessment criteria and methodology

Do you believe that the criteria for the Security Risk Assessments set out in Article 99 are sufficient? Should they be further developed or would you like to see a common risk methodology for non-technical risks applied to the security risk assessments?

Slovenia supports the development of a common EU methodology for assessing non-technical supply-chain risks. The methodology should address, inter alia, supplier ownership and control, exposure to third-country interference, legal obligations applicable in the supplier’s country of establishment, strategic dependencies, concentration risks, access to sensitive systems and data, supply continuity and the availability of alternative suppliers.

The methodology should clearly distinguish between the assessment of the ICT asset, the supply chain, the supplier and the possible mitigation measures. Slovenia also considers that the exceptional procedure under Article 99(3) requires clearer activation criteria, stronger Member State involvement, a limited duration and subsequent confirmation through the ordinary procedure.

Question 6 – Information and methodology for comitology

In the current proposal of Title IV, as part of the adoption of the implementing act, the comitology process is used to prohibit use of high-risk suppliers after the security risk assessment carried out as per Article 99 and the economic and societal impacts are assessed by the Commission as per Article 103(4). Does this process provide sufficient clarity, information and methodology for Member State experts to make a complete judgement during the comitology process?

Not fully. Slovenia considers that Member State experts must receive a standardised and sufficiently detailed decision package before voting. It should include the complete risk assessment, the evidence supporting the identification of the supplier, an assessment of dependencies and available alternatives, economic and societal impacts, migration costs, operational risks, proposed transition periods and an explanation of why less restrictive measures would not be sufficient.

Slovenia supports a binding and up-to-date EU list of high-risk suppliers to avoid inconsistent assessments by individual contracting authorities. Restrictions should be proportionate and accompanied by sector-specific transition periods, review and delisting procedures, rights of defence and effective judicial protection.
